Corruption impacts the health sector in many ways, from falsified medicines to rigged tenders and bribery. Health systems involve multiple actors, intricate supply chains and information asymmetries, making the sector complex and uniquely vulnerable to corruption. 
Despite the increasing recognition of the threat corruption poses to the health sector, there is a paucity of information at present on the extent to which corruption exists within the sector and the scale of its impact. 
Our work looks to remedy this. On behalf of the World Health Organization (WHO), we are developing a methodology to cost the global impact of corruption in the health sector. We will use the methodology to estimate the global cost of corruption, both in economic and health terms, and develop a national costing toolkit which will allow governments to estimate the impact of corruption and identify system vulnerabilities within their own health systems.
### Challenges
Corruption thrives in darkness, and a lack of clear information on the scale and nature of corruption makes it difficult to address. Despite recognition of its impact on health systems and the attainment of Universal Health Coverage (UHC), there have been very few attempts to quantify the true global scale of corruption. Further, there have been fewer attempts to identify system vulnerabilities and invest in anti-corruption initiatives. This work aims to redress these limitations and develop a comprehensive global estimate, alongside a toolkit that will allow global health organisations, government policymakers and anti-corruption advocates to have a clearer understanding of the impact and scale of corruption.
### Expertise
We are using a phased approach to develop a global costing methodology and national toolkit.
  * In the first phase, we will develop an understanding of differing estimation approaches, data sources and corruption typologies through a structured literature review. We will use key informant interviews to complement the evidence review and validate findings to ascertain further data sources. 
  * This will inform the development of a corruption costing engine that draws upon data sources to develop a global costing methodology. The global methodology will utilise a top-down modelling approach, drawing on multiple data sets to develop a model of estimated loss across key health system functions. To address potential data gaps, we will consider stratifying countries by categories (e.g. according to Human Development Index) and use imputation or regression approaches to address gaps. Recognising the uncertainty and potential lack of data on corruption, we will incorporate sensitivity analysis to generate uncertainty ranges around estimates. The model development will be guided by an independent expert working group, who will guide on methodological approaches, and data sources. 
  * The global costing engine will form the basis for national corruption costing toolkits, which will allow governments to model potential levels of corruption within their own systems. Proxy data, drawn from global modelling, will allow estimates to be produced in settings where data is lacking or where governments want to predict the impacts that policy changes may have on levels of corruption.


### Impact
The outcomes from this work will provide the empirical foundations to allow progress on tackling corruption in the health sector. Comprehensive estimates on corruption levels will remove the uncertainty that has hindered action for so long; an important step in ensuring corruption does not derail the global ambition of delivering Universal Health Coverage (UHC) for all by 2030.
More specifically:
At the global level, our work will help build momentum on tackling corruption by demonstrating the cost and impact that corruption has on the attainment of UHC. Global estimates on the impact of corruption, the first of their kind, will provide evidence and insights as to which parts of health systems are most vulnerable to corruption, providing a basis to advocate for action on tackling corruption. 
At the country level, national toolkits will allow governments to model where corruption vulnerabilities lie within their own systems. By identifying corruption vulnerabilities within national systems, governments will be able to develop more effective business cases to invest in anti-corruption measures, stemming vulnerabilities and increasing system effectiveness.
