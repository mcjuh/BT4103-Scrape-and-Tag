We are working with Asian Development Bank to support Vanuatu's Ministry of Health in designing and piloting a tailored training approach for village health workers. The project will help equip village health workers with the skills to tackle climate-related health risks and prevention of non-communicable diseases.

Vanuatu's Ministry of Health (MoH) relies on a decentralised health system, with Village Health Workers (VHWs) providing essential curative and preventive services at village level under the Healthy Islands Policy. In many remote communities, VHWs are the first, and often only point of contact with the health system.
Vanuatu faces two converging pressures: 
  * Climate change: the country is listed as one of the most vulnerable countries in the region to the ill-effects of climate change; and
  * Non-communicable diseases (NCDs): such as hypertension, and diabetes account for around three-quarters of deaths in the region and remain widely undiagnosed and untreated. 


These challenges necessitate stronger investment in health system strengthening via stronger climate risk preparedness and NCD awareness, especially at the grassroots level. However, VHW training has historically been fragmented, with limited national curricula and minimal focus on climate sensitive health risks, including NCD detection in climate vulnerable settings.
Asian Development Bank (ADB) and OPM are working collaboratively to support Vanuatu's MoH to design and pilot a tailored training approach for VHWs. The project draws on evidence synthesis, situational analysis, and close consultation with the MoH, provincial staff, and VHWs themselves, to build a climate resilient, NCD responsive primary health system, without over medicalising the VHW role, and with lessons that can transfer across the wider Pacific region.
#### Challenges
Vanuatu's frontline health workforce is overstretched and under equipped for an evolving risk landscape. Workforce shortages are severe, at 1.77 health workers per 1,000 people against the WHO recommended 4.5, and VHWs receive limited compensation for an already demanding role.
Training has traditionally been fragmented and skewed towards communicable disease and episodic care, leaving gaps in emergency preparedness, NCD detection, and climate health literacy. There are no clear national guidelines for coordination during climate related emergencies, and the health system depends heavily on external funding.
Globally, evidence shows that community health workers already carry excessive workloads, and Vanuatu is no exception. Adding new climate and NCD responsibilities risks overburdening VHWs further, raising real questions about task prioritisation, incentives, and role clarity. Neither the links between climate-related NCD risks nor broader climate–health and NCD priorities have yet been formal VHW training requirements.
The core challenge, therefore, is designing an intervention that builds VHWs’ capacity in above areas without over-medicalisation of their role and without adding to their workload.
#### Expertise
The project includes evidence synthesis and situational analysis of climate health and NCD training gaps among VHWs, drawing on literature review and qualitative interviews with the MoH, health providers, and other key partners to design and deliver a tailored climate-health and NCD training module. The module will be developed in collaboration with the MoH, through an interactive process, including a dedicated workshop with the key stakeholders. Gender Equality, Disability, and Social Inclusion (GEDSI) considerations will be embedded throughout the design and delivery. We emphasise local ownership rather than a purely didactic module, building on existing community trust and input, contextual nuance, and storytelling, so that new skills reinforce, rather than sideline, the VHW's established role as a trusted community first responder.
#### Impact
The project will equip Vanuatu VHWs with the practical skills and competence to identify and respond to both climate related health risks and NCDs, and interlinkages between the two, alongside generating a cohort of the trained VHW trainers who can sustain and scale this capability nationally.
Anticipated outcomes include improved community level preparedness and response, better detection of climate-sensitive NCDs, alongside clearer community engagement and referral pathways, and a robust roadmap for embedding this training within Vanuatu's national health, climate adaptation, and NCD control frameworks.
Longer term, this contributes to a more climate resilient and NCD responsive primary health system in Vanuatu, reduced vulnerability to climate sensitive health challenges and NCD related morbidity and mortality, and more equitable health outcomes. Lessons from the pilot will be transferable across the wider Pacific region, supporting resilience beyond Vanuatu alone.
This work reflects OPM's broader purpose of strengthening health systems and climate resilience in the places most exposed to both pressures, by working alongside government partners and frontline health workers to design solutions that are locally owned, evidence based, and genuinely deliverable, rather than imposed from outside.
