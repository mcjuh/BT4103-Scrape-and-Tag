# Elusive threats, elastic defense: Securing AI at scale
Conventional security models are failing as AI rapidly reshapes the enterprise, introducing new attack surfaces. A study in partnership with Palo Alto Networks.
[Download the insights (will open in a new tab)](https://www.ibm.com/downloads/documents/us-en/15db1aa4dab5ac54)[Subscribe for more insights from IBM IBV (will open in a new window)](https://www.ibm.com/thought-leadership/institute-business-value/en-us/newsletters)
AI is changing both the nature of business operations and the cybersecurity landscape. As organizations integrate AI systems and agents into day-to-day workflows, supporting human experts with new forms of digital labor, adversaries are creating new agentic threats. They are fast, relentless, and precise.
New global research from the IBM Institute for Business Value (IBM IBV) in partnership with Palo Alto Networks captures the new threat landscape and details how organizations must evolve so they can securely scale AI across the enterprise. Two-thirds (67%) of surveyed executives say their organization has been targeted in the past year by an AI-enabled cyberattack. 61% of leaders say their organization’s AI models, assets, or data have been compromised, our research finds. This aligns with growing evidence that cybercriminals are targeting AI infrastructure and operations.
As enterprises rush to scale AI, they often fail to see where they are exposed and underestimate the AI-specific risks confounding current governance models. Many AI agents are enabled using one-time authentication and inherited permissions that cross organizational boundaries. Based on implicit trust, this kind of deployment carries significant risk.
In this environment, enterprises are scrambling to figure out how best to update their security strategy. How should they prioritize AI investments to harden defenses while fueling growth? This research brief, based on a global survey of 1,000 C-level executives in 17 countries, offers a path forward for navigating the amorphous AI landscape.
> 61% of leaders say their organization’s AI models, assets, or data have been compromised.
### The AI battlefield
Today’s cyberthreats are evolving faster than the defenses built to keep them at bay, agree 70% of the executives in our study. Bots that once probed host domains for vulnerabilities are transforming into a fleet of AI malware agents. These automated adversaries are weaponizing zero day vulnerabilities, moving faster than even the most highly skilled human defenders.
Broadened AI usage across the enterprise is multiplying security risks. Half of executives (48%) report their organizations lack adequate protection for model inference and live AI usage, and 56% say the same about AI model development and training. Meanwhile, 72% of executives say they’re seeing a rise in unsanctioned AI use by employees.
The competitive cost of subpar AI security is significant. Organizations with a coordinated, multi-agent strategy expect a 42% higher ROI compared to organizations with no AI security strategy. Those making the leap to fully autonomous AI capabilities expect a 160% higher ROI, on average. In other words, enterprises armed with proper AI tools and techniques appear well-positioned to defeat the AI weapons arrayed against them. 
**The state of enterprise AI** Organizations are currently in the early stages of adopting AI at-scale. Each step up in maturity sees approximately one-third to one-half the rate of adoption 
### Dynamic defense through AI exposure management
If widespread AI adoption and security governance are in tension, then addressing the friction becomes an urgent business imperative. So how best can organizations leverage AI to increase the tempo of their security operations?
AI resilience starts with the ability to assess risks proactively, capture and analyze telemetry data in real-time, and act on threats in minutes given finite knowledge and limited context. That puts AI exposure management on the frontline of cybersecurity defense. In our research, 76% of executives say exposure management will be their organization’s primary method for unifying prevention, detection, and response activities by 2028. Put another way: With AI exposure management, autonomy can become a strategic advantage. Without it, autonomy will likely amplify risk.

The good news is that executives are increasingly prioritizing AI exposure management. They are looking to move beyond compliance reporting and incident response to proactive, continuous risk-based threat assessment. While only 38% of surveyed organizations say they had integrated AI exposure management into risk, detection and response solutions as of 2025, 70% expect to by 2028. 
### Making AI a platform for growth
While AI exposure management is the first step toward integrated, end-to-end AI security, next-generation defense against autonomous threats relies on a unified platform for managing shared data, identity controls, and governance.
Cybersecurity systems today are often far removed from that vision: a patchwork of limited visibility and operational disconnects that slow threat detection and response. In fact, organizations in our survey report having 27 different AI solutions on average, from 10 different vendors. Their average total number of cybersecurity solutions: 73, from 22 vendors. This level of complexity impedes efficient security operations.
A unified AI platform is ambient in nature, supporting end-to-end operations by integrating IT, security, applications, cloud services, and other functional areas. Leaders we spoke with see the merits in taking a platform approach to integrated AI operations. Most executives acknowledge the need for greater AI interoperability. Three out of four (77%) in our research say their organizations need better integration across hybrid cloud, AI, and security platforms. 
### Aligning the ecosystem to secure AI at scale
Given the reality that AI value derives from shared models, data, agents, and workflows, leaders need to assess AI operations in terms of shared responsibility. Interconnected AI infrastructure and operations demand that security be managed at an ecosystem level, bringing together partners that share responsibility for managing risk and ensuring accountability.
A large majority of surveyed executives (82%) agree that AI security is a shared responsibility across suppliers, providers, and partners. But four in 10 say their current approach to shared responsibility is either “minimally effective” or “not effective.” The three biggest obstacles? Complexity across multiple AI providers, rapid evolution of AI technologies, and limited visibility into AI provider security controls. 
### Secure AI leaders are emerging
Our analysis finds that when organizations integrate AI into their technology, security, and operations infrastructure, and when they adopt mature AI exposure management and shared governance practices, good things happen. Cybersecurity threats are resolved faster. The ROI on IT- and information security (IS)-related AI investments rises. And advantages derived from technology and security operations grow.
Many organizations are in different places on their AI integration and security journey—no surprise there. But our research finds that about one in four (24%) organizations have begun to break out from the pack in terms of autonomous AI readiness, exposure management, AI operation integration and shared governance. In contrast, more than one in four (28%) organizations are struggling to get started, and risk falling behind. 
### The path forward
Securing AI at scale requires new thinking and action on multiple fronts. Old security assumptions and practices need to be cast aside. New infrastructure needs to be built and integrated to power the shift to secure agentic and autonomous AI. And the relationship between humans and technology needs to be reimagined as machine-speed threats and digital labor models evolve.
Achieving greater security performance and resilience from AI investments is a multi-dimensional, enterprise-wide change process. The four interrelated factors are clear: Robust AI asset governance, an integrated AI-first operations and security environment, a mature AI exposure management posture, and a strategically aligned security ecosystem.
