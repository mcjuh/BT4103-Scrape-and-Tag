### What do we mean by ‘smart’ technical assistance?
The term ‘smart technical assistance’ (smart TA) has gained traction in the development sector in recent years – including in the [Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office’s (FCDO's) own guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/understanding-and-using-smart-technical-assistance/understanding-and-using-smart-technical-assistance-april-2025) on how to design and commission TA programmes. We welcome that framing. But for OPM, smart TA is not primarily a commissioning principle. It is a way of working – one we have developed and refined over decades of delivering deeply embedded, sustained reform programmes.  
| 
##### **OUR WORKING DEFINITION**
Smart TA is context-aware, politically informed, and partnership-driven support that uses shared diagnostics, co-creation, and adaptive real-time learning to strengthen locally-owned systems – supporting the design and delivery of reforms that build sustainable institutional capability for long-term impact.  |  
| --- |  
Here we set out what we mean by smart TA: where our model comes from, what it looks like in practice, and what distinguishes it. 
### Where our model comes from
Our approach to smart TA has not been designed in the abstract. It has developed from doing the work – from years of learning, and close enduring partnerships with governments tackling genuinely difficult institutional reform: public financial management, subsidy reform, climate mainstreaming, economic governance, service delivery transformation.
Over many years of supporting systems reform, we have developed an approach to technical assistance that is embedded within government institutions; politically literate, adaptive, and relentlessly focused on the delivery of results. Our experience of delivering the[ Sub-National Governance (SNG) programme](https://www.opml.co.uk/insights/when-technical-assistance-delivers-results-pakistans-sub-national-governance-programme) in Pakistan has been especially influential, and helped shape FCDO’s thinking on what effective TA looks like. We are proud of that legacy - and are focused on building on it.
Our experience implementing other reform programmes across South Asia, Africa, and beyond have reinforced the same lessons: that the difference between TA that leaves a mark and TA that does not is rarely about the technical quality of the advice. It is about how that advice is delivered, owned, adapted, and institutionalised.
### What smart TA is – and what it is not 
Before setting out what we mean by smart TA, it is worth being clear about what it is not. These are patterns we recognise – and actively try to avoid. Smart TA is not:
  * Short-term, isolated inputs with no follow-through
  * Producing a report, strategy or policy that government did not ask for, does not own, and will not implement 
  * Substituting for government capacity rather than building it
  * Transplanting a best-practice blueprint designed elsewhere without adapting it to context
  * Working only within the narrow scope of a log frame when the reform requires something different 


> "Smart TA isn’t a blueprint. You can’t transplant a model from one context and expect it to take root in another. You have to build it on site."
Defining what smart TA is not is the easier task – and the patterns described above are rarely the result of bad intentions. No one sets out to deliver TA that substitutes for government capacity or produces strategies that gather dust. Yet too often, that is where programmes land. The harder question is what makes TA work – and the honest answer is that it cannot be reduced to a replicable set of principles alone. Context changes, politics shifts, windows open and close. What smart TA demands, above all, is the expert judgment to read a situation and select the right approach, the relationships to make it work, and the discipline to stay focused on outcomes rather than outputs. 
### The elements of OPM’s smart TA model
We have distilled our experience into eight interconnected elements. These are not a sequential checklist – in practice, they operate simultaneously and iteratively. But together, they describe what it means to deliver TA that is genuinely smart.
##### Table 1: OPM’s smart TA model   
| 
###### Model element
 | 
###### Description
 |  
| --- | --- |  
| **Shared diagnosis**  | We start by understanding deep-seated problems – often going beyond the Terms of Reference. Diagnosis is always a joint exercise, conducted with relevant counterparts, drawing on political economy analysis and an honest read of what is and is not achievable in the current context.   |  
| **Political and contextual awareness**  | Reform does not happen in a vacuum. We work with the grain of incentives, power structures, and political economy rather than against them. We read the context continuously and adjust. A technically perfect solution that nobody can or will implement is not a solution.   |  
| **Knowing when to do and when to enable**  | Smart TA requires judgments about roles and engagement. Sometimes the right intervention is direct – drafting the strategy, modelling the numbers, writing the policy. Other times it is facilitation, coaching, or backstopping government to do it themselves. The skill lies in knowing when to use each approach, and being honest about it.   |  
| **Boundary spanning**  | Complex reform rarely stays within the jurisdiction of a single entity, for example a ministry or department. We routinely work across institutional boundaries – connecting subnational to national, government to non-government, often engaging at the highest levels of political leadership – to build coalitions for change that reform requires. Within government, the creation of cross-departmental ‘reform working groups’ has ensured joint ownership of reforms.   |  
| **Adaptive real-time learning**  | We treat log frames and theories of change as starting points, not straitjackets. When context shifts – when a minister changes, an opportunity opens, or an approach is not working – we adapt. We build feedback loops into how we work, so that learning from implementation ensures what we do remains relevant.   |  
| **Supporting implementation, not just conception**  | Historically, international TA has often involved advising and leaving – producing strategies and plans that stall at implementation. We have moved deliberately beyond this. We work alongside government and local partners throughout the process: from diagnosis and design, to implementation, real-time learning and adaptation, using tools such as roadmaps to ensure reforms actually deliver results.   |  
| **Building for durability**  | Reforms that depend on external TA to survive are not reforms. Our goal is to leave behind capability, routines, coalitions, and – where possible – formal institutional anchors (laws, rules, structures, budget allocations) that persist after we exit. Sustainability is not a handover activity; it is designed in from the start.   |  
| **The right people, with the right relationships**  | None of the above works without the right team. Smart TA requires people who combine technical credibility with political literacy, genuine relationships with counterparts, and the attitude to serve local goals rather than their own. This is often what separates programmes that work from those that do not.   |  
### The underlying condition: empathy
These eight elements describe what smart TA does. But there is a prior question: what makes it possible to do them well?
In our experience, the answer is empathy – not as a soft skill, but as a professional discipline. It means genuinely understanding what the recipient needs and can reasonably accommodate, the political pressures they are navigating, and the institutional constraints they cannot escape. It means understanding the gap between what they are being asked to do, and what they actually have the power to change. Without that depth of understanding, shared diagnosis becomes a box-ticking exercise, political awareness becomes a checklist, and adaptive learning becomes reactive firefighting.
This kind of insight takes time, relationships, and a genuine orientation towards the people you are working with – not just the problem you have been contracted to solve. It is, in our view, what separates smart transformative TA from performative TA that merely goes through the motions.
### Who we are working for
Every OPM programme operates within a stakeholder triangle: a funding client (typically a donor such as FCDO), a recipient client (the government or local institution we are supporting), and the wider population that will ultimately benefit, or not.
In our experience, the most important discipline in smart TA is clarity about whose interests come first. Our answer is unambiguous: the objective of the TA is to serve the recipient client. Ultimately, this is in service of the third point of the stakeholder triangle: the people whose lives reform is meant to improve. Keeping government at the centre is not an end in itself – it is the most reliable route to delivering change that actually reaches them.
This does not mean we disregard funders – we take our contractual obligations and funder relationships seriously. But when the funder’s diagnosis and recipient needs diverge, we advocate for the latter. We will constructively challenge Terms of Reference, request scope adjustments, and use our position as an honest broker between government and donor when that is what is needed.
This orientation matters practically. It is what earns trust with government counterparts, makes our advice credible, and enables the kind of embedded, long-term partnership in which smart TA can take root and deliver.
### Smart TA in practice: an example 
In Pakistan, the Sub-National Governance programme, funded by FCDO, provided embedded support to federal and provincial governments across critical reform areas such as public financial management, pension reform, social protection, and solid waste management. A sub-component of SNG – funded through the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) – is a perfect illustration of TA in action.
A small team, working within the Prime Minister’s Office, worked with government ministries to design and drive economic transformation through sectoral reform roadmaps across eleven ministries. There was no separate log frame for this work, or blueprint. The roadmaps were context-specific, shaped by what government needed, adapted as priorities shifted, and used by FCDO to inform its own country strategy. The reform process itself – not the TA contract – became the organising framework.
This is what smart TA looks like when it is working. 
### Smart TA is evolving – and so are we
The direction of travel in international development is clear: towards greater local ownership, genuine partnership, and a more honest reckoning with what external technical assistance can and cannot do. Across the sector – from FCDO's shift towards a partnership model, to the EU's growing appetite for flexible programme management, to the World Bank's own evolving thinking on implementation support – there is a growing recognition that TA which ignores local context and agency tends not to deliver. The technocratic, blueprint-driven approaches that characterised much TA provision in earlier decades are increasingly hard to defend.
We are well placed in this environment – not because we have perfected smart TA, but because we have been practising it, reflecting on it, and refining it for a long time. The model described here is not a finished product. It is a living framework, shaped by delivery experience and improved with every programme we run.
However smart our TA becomes, the measure that matters most remains the simplest: whether the reforms we support deliver tangible improvements in people's lives.
