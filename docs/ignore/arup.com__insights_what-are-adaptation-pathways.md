#  What are adaptation pathways? 
Adaptation pathways anticipate and define changing circumstances, emergent risks and new priorities.

As many countries prioritise investments in infrastructure – across energy, transport, digital, flood defences and water systems – the task of making sound, long-term predictions about the future is becoming harder and harder.
This, in turn, is making investment decision-making more complex than ever. There’s a clear and growing need to avoid “lock-in” to solutions that may ultimately fail to deliver a safe, low carbon, nature positive and regenerative future. Adaptive planning – which produces adaptation pathways – is a means of tackling this challenge. When infrastructure designers focus on resilience, there are core principles that are universal across all forms of infrastructure: flexibility, responsiveness, robustness, redundancy, diversity, coordination and information. To overcome this considerable complexity (and the hesitancy it can cause), designers and engineers now develop solutions with adaptation pathways embedded within the form and implementation of a scheme or programme. 
This is a methodology that acknowledges that things do not always turn out as anticipated. Instead, by planning a for a range of foreseeable futures, it provides decision-makers with far greater flexibility than traditional planning methods. The outcome from an adaptive planning process is usually an adaptation pathway, including multiple sequences of actions, with alternative routes, path dependencies and lock-in risks, providing a route through decisions over time. 
Adaptation pathways anticipate and define changing circumstances, emergent risks and new priorities. Fundamentally, this is a highly practical decision-making framework that helps organisations to: • Build clear, defensible investment cases • Phase their investments or spending over time • Maintain flexibility when new realities or priorities emerge• Reduce wasted or unwise investments in key infrastructure
Adaptation pathways for the water sector
Adaptive planning is extremely relevant for communities and organisations that need to make decisions about the future of the water infrastructure. Rather than committing to a single fixed solution, pathways set out a flexible sequence of actions over time that are linked to defined triggers such as regulatory changes, population growth, asset performance or climate change impacts. This results in a stepwise approach to investment and delivery that prioritises the delivery of low-regret actions that deliver immediate benefits while keeping future options open. As new information emerges, plans can be adjusted in a structured and transparent way, allowing strategies to stay agile without losing direction.
This approach helps organisations avoid premature or unnecessary spend and improve value for money while building long-term resilience across interconnected water systems. It also creates a clear, risk-based narrative for decision-makers to support funding, regulatory engagement, and stakeholder confidence.
Ultimately, by embracing adaptation pathways organisations transition from a reactionary approach to actively managing uncertainty, ultimately leading to more resilient water systems.
Adaptation pathways for flood and coastal resilience
Managing the risks posed by flooding and coastal erosion is another complex challenge where adaptive planning can play a role. The approach requires deep collaboration between all parties involved, to agree values and priorities, governance approaches, credible scenarios and desired outcomes. Given the wide range of climate change predictions, uncertainties over the performance of ageing assets and the many other factors that affect decision-making (including funding, policy, societal changes), adaptation pathways are particularly useful because they anticipate a range of possible futures, allowing the formulation of placed-based approach to risk and uncertainty.
Adaptation pathways encompass the local evidence base and profile the risks communities already face, backed by a thorough understanding of asset health and the value at risk. This allows flood and coastal risk managers to deliver capital and maintenance works in the short term, while supporting a longer-term adaptation programme. This level of flexibility enables money and resources to be spent effectively, and a climate change response through monitoring and evaluation, so that the effectiveness of the measures can be better understood and decisions refined.
Adaptation pathways for the energy sector
For a local energy system to be resilient and reliable will depend on both how well it meets today’s energy demand (in its different forms), and growing demand driven by changing patterns of generation and consumption. Plainly, an electrical grid infrastructure built for a world of a few tens of centralised, remotely located coal-fired power stations will not be well suited to a world of tens of thousands of solar and wind generation assets. And a system built around a temperate climate with a winter energy demand peak will have to adapt to a future where rising temperatures drive summer peaks from cooling demand.
Once more, we can see how an adaptive planning approach to investment enables energy infrastructure operators and investors to model future pathways of change and to identify the required changes in system performance and capability adaptations to keep the lights on every day. By identifying the interim steps towards a future system which is adapted to a projected future state (future population, future climate, future resources etc.), planners can identify what needs to be built now and what can be added in the future.
Adaptation pathways for the transport sector
Transport network owners and operators in many countries have to face the reality of ageing transportation infrastructure, where elements of road, rail and maritime networks have been in place since the 19th century. Given the common challenge of funding constraints, the full cost to adapt legacy infrastructure to be resilient to projected climatic extremes through the 21st century is rarely practical. An adaptation pathway is a great way to codify a more practical and flexible approach to upgrades and expansions, supporting decisions with the latest climate science and models, and understanding of condition and performance and adopting a mix of short term (e.g. surface repairs or temporary road closures) and long term (e.g. natural flood management, or elevation of road/rail sections) solutions.
This helps owners and operators to prioritise interventions that will tangible and vital aspects of modernisation without being paralysed into action by the ultimate scale of funding and programme.
