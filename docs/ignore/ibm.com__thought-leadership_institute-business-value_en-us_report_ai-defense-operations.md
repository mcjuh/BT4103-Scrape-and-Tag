# Defense and Intelligence in the AI era
Perspectives from NATO and Five Eyes leaders: AI is changing the readiness landscape for defense and intelligence.
[Download the insights (will open in a new tab)](https://www.ibm.com/downloads/documents/us-en/16951f13a461725d)[Subscribe for more insights from IBM IBV (will open in a new window)](https://www.ibm.com/thought-leadership/institute-business-value/en-us/newsletters)
### AI is moving ahead but Defense and Intelligence systems are still catching up
NATO and Five Eyes nations, military leaders say artificial intelligence is taking on a much larger role in how their organizations plan, assess threats, and operate. They expect AI to influence tasks ranging from intelligence analysis and cyber defense to logistics resilience and multi‑domain coordination. But there’s a gap between expectation and execution: only 10% of leaders say their organizations are highly successful with AI today, and most defense organizations still can’t use AI inside the systems that determine daily decisions.
That tension comes through in a survey of 600 NATO and Five Eyes CIOs and CTOs responsible for AI strategy across ministries, services, joint commands, and major defense agencies. Their message is consistent: interest in AI is high, investment is growing, but progress varies widely. 
### AI spending is rising fast—but results are slower to appear
Defense leaders are increasing their AI budgets, with many expecting spending to more than double by 2028. Much of that money is flowing into data readiness—improving data quality, management, and governance. Leaders often describe this foundational work as essential, since advanced capabilities don’t function reliably without it.
There’s also growing exploration of generative AI and the early formation of agent‑based systems designed to assist with tasks like automated reporting, threat detection, and mission planning support. Agentic AI spending alone is expected to more than double, reflecting its potential to coordinate workflows with limited prompting. 
**On average, AI budget allocations are expected to shift away from data and traditional AI and into generative and agentic AI by 2028**
But across high‑priority mission areas such as cyber operations, logistics, and command and control, adoption remains dominated by evaluations and pilot projects. In most mission areas, 60–70% of organizations are still evaluating or piloting AI; fully embedded systems are still the exception. Even in functional areas like IT operations, cybersecurity, maintenance, and supply chain management, pilots continue to outnumber scaled deployments.
The pattern reflects a hard truth: turning high‑performing prototypes into tools that work inside decades‑old systems is a much harder lift than building the prototypes themselves. 
### The real bottleneck? Systems that aren’t ready for AI
When defense technology leaders explain why AI remains stuck at the edges, they point to the systems expected to use them.
> “Modernizing legacy systems is essential, as many current platforms limit our ability to use AI effectively and at scale.”
> Chief Information OfficerCombat Arms Unit, France
Data quality is a challenge. Leaders estimate that only about half of their data is useful for AI, and only one‑third has been prepared to a usable standard. But even organizations that have made strides on data readiness still struggle to achieve operational impact.
The more decisive issue is system readiness. Leaders report that fewer than one‑third of their core systems are AI‑ready—meaning they cannot absorb AI‑generated insights or automate actions. Most command‑and‑control systems, logistics tools, sustainment platforms, and intelligence applications were never designed to ingest machine‑generated outputs. As a result, AI might generate valuable recommendations, but those insights often cannot route into the workflows where they would matter.
Acquisition timelines add friction. More than 60% of leaders say procurement needs to move at least twice as fast to keep up with evolving AI capabilities. That slow pace creates strategic vulnerability, especially when adversaries face fewer procedural constraints.
Talent is another factor. Many organizations lean heavily on vendors to stand up early projects. But leaders say internal expertise—people who understand both the mission and the technology—is essential for reliability, resilience, and long‑term scale.
Together, these constraints explain why broad experimentation has not yet translated into widespread operational use. 
### Early AI gains are coming from enterprise and support functions
One of the clearest patterns emerging from the data is where AI is actually gaining traction—the enterprise and support functions that keep defense organizations running.
Areas like IT operations, cybersecurity monitoring, maintenance, procurement, and supply chain forecasting are seeing more consistent progress. These functions typically have more structured data, clearer processes, and modernization initiatives already underway—making them better positioned to integrate AI into daily workflows.
Mission pilots, meanwhile, often remain isolated from the systems that must ultimately act on their outputs. They can perform impressively in controlled environments but face significant hurdles when tied to real operational systems.
Leaders say this functional progress matters. It helps build the technical and organizational foundations needed before AI can reliably influence mission‑level decision making. 
### National infrastructure is becoming part of the AI equation
As AI becomes more embedded in defense, leaders are increasingly framing the challenge as one of national infrastructure. They point to data sovereignty, cybersecurity, resilient networks, sovereign compute, and domestic talent development as key to long‑term readiness.
Two priorities rise consistently to the top: ensuring national control over sensitive data and guaranteeing secure access to compute. Two‑thirds of leaders cite data sovereignty and cybersecurity as their top national priorities. They emphasize the need for AI‑enabled systems that can withstand cyberattacks, supply chain disruptions, or geopolitical pressure. 
**National AI resilience/sovereignty priorities**
Nations differ in where they focus first. Those with more mature digital ecosystems put greater emphasis on governance, assurance, and standards. Others are prioritizing foundational security—data control, secure cloud environments, and compute resilience. But across the board, leaders acknowledge that uneven AI maturity within alliances can complicate joint operations and introduce risk.
There is also a notable divide in how leaders weigh the risks. Nearly 60% believe the greater danger is falling behind adversaries, while others stress the importance of guardrails and governance. That tension underscores a central challenge: how to accelerate adoption while maintaining trust and control. 
### The path forward: building the foundations AI needs
Across conversations with leaders, a consistent theme emerges: AI’s future impact depends on improving the systems and structures beneath it, not simply deploying more advanced models.
**Organizations highlight several priorities:**
  * Modernize the systems where core decisions and operations occur
  * Treat data quality and governance as ongoing responsibilities
  * Develop internal expertise capable of sustaining long‑term adoption
  * Push pilots into real workflows instead of keeping them on the edges
  * Ensure human accountability and oversight stay central as autonomy increases


**At the national level, leaders emphasize:**
  * Sovereign control over data and compute
  * Strengthening digital and AI infrastructure to withstand disruption
  * Expanding domestic talent pipelines
  * Building governance frameworks that support rapid, responsible adoption
  * Ensuring interoperability with allies while maintaining sovereignty


> “Sovereign AI systems must remain interoperable with allies while preserving national control.”
> Chief Information OfficerCombat Arms Unit, Netherlands
Across NATO and Five Eyes nations, the trajectory is clear. Defense organizations see AI’s growing role and are investing accordingly. The work ahead is about building readiness—upgrading systems, securing data, developing talent, and ensuring AI can operate reliably when it’s needed most. 
**FAQ**
How are defense and intelligence organizations using AI today?
Most AI use remains in pilots, with growing activity in areas like cyber defense, logistics, and intelligence analysis. 
How much are defense and intelligence organizations investing in AI?
Leaders expect AI spending to more than double within three years, with the largest share going to data readiness. 
How much progress have defense and intelligence organizations made with AI?
Only about 10% of leaders say their organizations are highly successful today, despite rising investment. 
Where is AI delivering value in defense right now?
Early gains are strongest in enterprise functions such as IT operations, cybersecurity monitoring, maintenance, and supply chain forecasting. 
What do defense and intelligence leaders consider the top priorities for AI resilience?
They cite data sovereignty, cybersecurity, and secure access to compute as the most critical national foundations.
