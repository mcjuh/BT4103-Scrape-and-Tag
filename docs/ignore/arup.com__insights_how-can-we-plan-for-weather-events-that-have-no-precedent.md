#  How can we plan for weather events that have no precedent? 
The world needs resilient infrastructure – the energy, transport, water and data networks civilisation depends on. So how do we design for the future when predicting intensifying climate impacts is proving so hard?
Steve Downie
Associate Director

As humans, we are always seeking ways to manage the world around us and the risks it presents. We observe patterns in the past to enable us predict the future – if not precisely, then at least in the sense that we understand what sort of events are possible and how likely they are within a given timeframe. This approach is foundational to managing risk of extreme climatic events, as we develop, operate and maintain vital infrastructure.
When it comes to extreme events, we have developed sophisticated mathematical frameworks to inform this sort of forecast. Whether you’re an engineer, worried about the likelihood of structural failure over an asset life, or an insurer, trying to price a policy against expected losses, we expect to be able to speak in the hard language of numbers about things like return periods and annual probabilities. We believe we possess a meaningful level of certainty in making these predictions.
Human induced climate change challenges this – if we can’t trust that the patterns we see in the past will persist in the future, then forecasting risk becomes a fraught undertaking. Thankfully we have a vast, almost overwhelming, quantity of data from global climate models, coordinated by the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP), and a host of related datasets with a regional focus. These data have provided an obvious route to adapt our climate hazard models to accommodate the continuous, steady drift towards a warmer world while retaining the language of numerical probabilities.
But what if the process isn’t continuous? What if it’s _discontinuous_? What if we’re observing a rupture with the past, where a new world, which behaves in ways for which there is no precedent, is emerging? How should we plan for a future when the patterns may have shifted in ways we cannot predict and risk due to extreme events may not be quantifiable? 
## Making plans in a time of radical change
Forecasting probabilities of extreme weather events in future climates is a controversial topic. One view is that we could make confident estimates if we were able to run sufficiently large numbers of models at sufficiently high spatial resolution, such that we capture the important physical processes while also rolling the metaphorical dice enough times to ensure that even very rare events are represented somewhere in the dataset. Unfortunately, the computational resources required for this are beyond even the leading international climate research centres at present.
Adding another layer of complexity on top, in which extreme weather is occurring against a backdrop where one or more large scale climate systems – e.g. the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Current (AMOC) – has undergone a qualitative shift, is even more daunting. Yet this is exactly what is expected from developers of nationally significant energy infrastructure in the UK. To quote directly from the Overarching National Policy Statement for Energy (EN-1):
_“The Secretary of State should be satisfied that there are not features of the design of new energy infrastructure critical to its operation which may be seriously affected by more radical changes to the climate beyond that projected in the latest set of UK climate projections, taking account of the latest credible scientific evidence.”_
Having recently led a review of related guidance for nuclear duty-holders on behalf of the Office for Nuclear Regulation (ONR) in the UK, I have returned to this statement often. For nuclear power plants, the combination of long design lives and high potential consequences of failure should make the need for this sort of worst-case scenario planning obvious, but it remains a challenging question to answer.
## Definitions for new challenges
Firstly, what is the threshold for “radical” and what does it mean to be “beyond the latest set of UK climate projections”. ONR’s regulatory guidance draws a distinction between reasonably foreseeable effects of climate change, which should be considered in the design basis, and credible maximum scenarios, under which the duty-holder is expected to demonstrate that adaptation measures could be put in place in a timely manner to maintain safe operation if required.
Given a return period of 1 in 10,000 years is the norm for design basis events in the nuclear sector, this isn’t just about low likelihood. ONR further clarify this by defining a credible maximum as “peer-reviewed, high-end, and **_plausible_** climate change projections”. It’s a slippery concept though, which is best described by way of example.
## Familiar threats: sea level rise
The below image is taken from [IPCC AR6](https://www.ipcc.ch/assessment-report/ar6/), it shows a bunch of solid lines, within shaded bands and spanning an approximate range of half a meter to a meter of global sea level rise up to 2100. This spread of predictions could be said to represent the reasonably foreseeable impacts of climate change. Most of the uncertainty is driven by the fact we don’t know the human factor will play out in terms of emissions scenarios – the most pessimistic scenario is SSP5-8.5, which has recently been “retired” as it appears increasingly implausible as the energy transition gathers pace. There is room to manoeuvre therefore but it would be imprudent not to pick a number somewhere in this range and plan around it.
Image credit: IPCC AR6 
The elephant in the room is the dotted line, which sits well above the ‘reasonably foreseeable’ band. In this scenario – referred to as the low-likelihood, high impact storyline – the impacts from feedback mechanisms driving accelerated ice sheet melt off are included. The decision to exclude these mechanisms from the other projections is largely based on the fact we simply don’t have a good enough scientific grasp of the processes yet and any estimates are therefore subject to deep uncertainty.
It is very difficult to say just how likely the low-likelihood, high-impact storyline is but, importantly, it is considered physically plausible. As such, while it might not be reasonable to base your plans around it, it would certainly make sense to consider how your plans might need to change if it starts looking likely. It’s clearly valuable to explore how this for-now-hypothetical risk might inform adaptation strategies and understand the trigger points and timelines for putting those adaptations into place. 
## New risk horizons
This new kind of knowledge/prediction dilemma illustrates the type of thinking involved but only takes us part of the way to answering the ultimate question. Defining a credible maximum sea level is a good start, but it doesn’t tell us how the asset will be impacted and whether safety can be maintained. This requires an engineering assessment based on multiple parameters – storm surge, wave height, wind speed – all of which are driven by large scale climate processes which are unlikely to behave in the way we currently understand in a world where the sea level has risen by two meters.
For reasons discussed above, it would be prohibitively expensive (if even possible), to meet the regulator’s expectations using the probabilistic approaches we normally apply to climate risk assessments. Even if we could follow this approach for a single hazard, we are faced with a multi-dimensional spectrum of possibilities once we start thinking about combinations of different environmental actions and it becomes impossible to screen them all to quantify risk of a particular system threshold being exceeded.
## Climate storylines
Thankfully, there is precedent to draw on from other sectors on how to approach this type of problem. International financial systems are regularly subject to unexpected shocks that are complex in nature and can lead to severe, cascading consequences via points of vulnerability that were not previously understood. These types of risk are also hard to apply probabilistic approaches to. Instead, the industry deploys stress testing exercises using narrative scenarios, designed by panels of experts, based on real world events. This has emerged as an effective way to identify possible points of failure and assess overall system resilience by collectively gaming out a response to the hypothetical event.
A similar concept – referred to as “climate storylines” – is now gaining prominence for assessment of extreme weather events, with or without interwoven climate change impacts. Further development of the storylines approach was flagged in the third UK Climate Change Risk Assessment (CCRA3) as a key focus for research, with further prominent references to it appearing in the CCRA4 technical report. The upcoming IPCC Seventh Assessment Report (AR7) is also expected to feature a heavier focus on use of storylines to explore the impact of extreme climate scenarios.
A key feature of the storylines concept is co-development of the resulting scenarios between a broad range of stakeholders – asset owners/operators, engineers, meteorologists, climate scientists – with the goal of starting from an understanding of a particular vulnerability or decision point before working backwards to design an event to provide an appropriate stress test. This is in stark contrast to the prevailing approach, where scientific information is handed down from on high, with limited reference to the sometimes highly specific needs of the end user.
Such a collaborative approach can also involve input from the public and can therefore be a powerful means of communicating risk in digestible terms, while offering greater transparency around how climate change related risks are considered when planning nationally critical infrastructure.
For many, the extreme scenarios that need to be considered for nuclear infrastructure – e.g. AMOC collapse – might seem too far in the future to be relevant or disproportionate for the level of risk being managed. However, there is increasing evidence that the level of risk we are living under is already underappreciated for some types of extremes and a qualitative shift away from the predictable world of the past is already underway.
Throughout North America, Europe, China – all northern latitudes where weather patterns are subject to the dynamics of the Polar Jet Stream – we have seen a pattern of recurring long return period droughts, heatwaves and floods that increasingly looks different from historical patterns, not just shifted, different. Global climate models also seem to be underpredicting the associated effects. It’s still early days from a statistical perspective but already this year we have already seen heatwaves in the Western United States and Europe that were astonishing for the time of year and a further heatwave of potentially historic proportions is, at the time of writing, developing over the US eastern seaboard.
In the case of the earlier heatwave in the US, the unseasonable warmth has compounded low snowpack levels that will place enormous stress on water systems. Planning is no doubt taking place across a range of public and private organisations to manage the knock-on effects but, given the scenario is bordering on improbable from a statistical perspective, it is unlikely that anything similar has been considered in the past and the task is significantly more difficult as a result.
## Conclusion
As we approach a time in which most acknowledge the need to fundamentally transform all of our critical global systems, we have deep, far-reaching decisions to take as societies. Given the uncertainties over how our climate may change over the coming decades, adaptive resilience is becoming more and more of a necessary quality and, as a result, we need to spend more time fleshing out our understanding of plausible worst-case scenarios and the sort of extreme events that they may give rise to.

It a time of great change, we cannot predict the future with confidence but investing time pondering how we could manage our way through a broad range of worst-case scenarios puts us in a better position to make smart adaptations under the pressure of unfolding shocks and stresses. Climate storylines offer a structured route towards this goal while, critically, enabling public engagement with the decision-making process.To quote former U.S. President Eisenhower:
“Plans are worthless, but planning is everything.”
