# Tokenized ETFs 101: What they are and why they matter
  * Blog
  * July 08, 2026


Roland Kastoun 
Asset and Wealth Management Advisory Leader, PwC US
Bill Donahue 
Managing Director, Assurance, US Asset and Wealth Management, PwC US
#### Key takeaways:
  * ETF tokenization is coming closer to reality, yet it poses several difficult questions that market participants need to answer to set their strategy in this new area.
  * This article briefly covers operational complexity, infrastructure readiness and economic incentives.
  * The immediate path forward isn't about replacing traditional infrastructure overnight. It's about thoughtfully building bridges between existing systems and new technologies.


The ETF industry is on the cusp of its next major evolution: tokenization. This technology could fundamentally transform how we trade, settle, and manage investment funds. We recently issued [Minting the future: Why asset tokenization is accelerating](https://www.pwc.com/us/en/industries/financial-services/library/tokenizing-assets.html) and spoke at the Investment Company Institute conference. The industry is clearly beginning the journey to tokenization, yet fundamental questions remain, including what is a tokenized ETF, and why should investors and industry professionals care?
##  What is a tokenized ETF?
A tokenized ETF is a traditional ETF whose shares are represented as digital tokens on a blockchain or distributed ledger. While the underlying investment strategy remains the same, the ETF’s share trading infrastructure changes dramatically.
Traditional ETFs rely on brokerage accounts, centralized clearinghouses like the DTCC, and T+1 settlement cycles. Tokenized ETFs, by contrast, utilize immutable digital ledgers and smart contracts, potentially enabling near-instant settlement and round-the-clock trading.
##  The promise of tokenization
Blockchain technology offers several compelling benefits for the exchange-traded fund ecosystem:
**Instant settlement:** Instead of waiting until the next business day (T+1), tokenized ETFs could enable "atomic settlement"—where ownership transfer and payment occur simultaneously. This dramatically reduces counterparty risk and capital requirements.
**24/7 trading:** Blockchain networks operate continuously, allowing investors to trade ETFs anytime, including weekends and holidays.
**Global distribution:** Tokenization may simplify cross-border access to investment products, potentially opening funds to global investors without friction from international securities trading.
**Cost efficiencies:** Smart contracts can automate many functions currently handled by intermediaries, potentially reducing operational costs throughout the investment chain.
**Transparency and auditability:** Every transaction on a blockchain creates an immutable record, improving compliance capabilities and building investor confidence through unprecedented transparency.
**Programmability:** Smart contracts can automate corporate actions like dividend distributions and compliance checks, streamlining processes that require significant manual intervention.
##  Critical questions and concerns
Despite the promise, the industry faces significant hurdles before realizing the benefits.
**Operational complexity:** How will the creation and redemption processes— fundamental to ETF functioning—work in a tokenized environment? How will tokenized ETFs interact with traditional exchange-listed products during the transition period?
**Infrastructure readiness:** There's no industry consensus on whether public or private blockchains are appropriate for handling equity market order flows. The infrastructure supporting traditional markets has evolved over decades; tokenized systems must prove equally robust.
**Regulatory uncertainty:** Questions abound regarding SEC requirements, shareholder voting structures, and maintaining investor protections in this new paradigm. Regulators must balance encouraging innovation while safeguarding market integrity.
**Fraud and security risks:** The speed of blockchain settlement could potentially outpace quality control processes, creating opportunities for bad actors. Security frameworks must evolve accordingly.
**Economic incentives:** Will broker-dealers and other intermediaries have sufficient financial motivation to adopt and support these new products? Without their buy-in, adoption will stall.
**Investor demand:** Is there a genuine market appetite for tokenized assets, or is this a solution seeking a problem? The industry must demonstrate clear value propositions for end investors.
##  Stakeholders
This conversation involves diverse participants with different motivations:
**Asset managers** see opportunities for enhanced distribution, reduced operational costs, and differentiated product offerings.
**Blockchain and fintech companies** are working to bring traditional finance "on-chain," positioning themselves as critical infrastructure providers for the future.
**Regulators** face the delicate task of fostering innovation while protecting investors and maintaining market stability.
**Intermediaries** —broker-dealers, custodians, and transfer agents—must assess the economic viability and necessary operational changes to their business models.
**Investors** will ultimately determine success by evaluating whether tokenized products offer meaningful benefits over traditional ones.
##  Looking ahead
Tokenization represents an evolution, not a revolution. Realizing its full potential requires solving complex challenges that span operations, regulation, and economics.
For instance, the benefits of instant settlement are limited if the underlying securities themselves aren't tokenized. Similarly, tokenized ETFs must integrate into existing collateral and pledging frameworks that support lending and margin activities.
The immediate path forward isn't about replacing traditional infrastructure overnight. It's about thoughtfully building bridges between existing systems and new technologies, ensuring that innovation serves investors while maintaining the market stability.
In the coming installments of this series, we'll dive deeper into the specifics: operations and technology (including creation/redemption processes), market mechanics and economics, regulatory frameworks, and governance considerations.
The tokenization of ETFs isn't science fiction—it's actively being developed and debated across the industry. Understanding these fundamentals now will prepare you for the changes ahead.
