# Where AI breaks–or breaks through
The human operating model that powers performance.
[Download the insights (will open in a new tab)](https://www.ibm.com/downloads/documents/us-en/16ddd5a57a3948f3)[Subscribe for more insights from IBM IBV (will open in a new window)](https://www.ibm.com/thought-leadership/institute-business-value/en-us/newsletters)
Two organizations can deploy the same AI into the same workflows—and see completely different results. The difference isn’t the technology. It’s the operating model they build around it—and the culture that takes shape from how work actually gets done. Across industries, AI is advancing faster than organizations can adapt. Nearly two-thirds of executives say AI is already reshaping roles and workflows. Yet seven in 10 report unclear decision rights and accountability—gaps that don’t just slow execution, but shape how people behave. That’s the real shift underway. AI isn’t just a technology transformation. It’s a cultural one—because every decision about how AI is used becomes a signal about how work is expected to happen.
> “This is an implementation challenge on steroids. The gap between the way things work today and the way they need to work tomorrow is much bigger than we’ve seen with past technologies.” 
> **Atul Bhardwaj** Executive Vice President and Chief Digital & Technology Officer, LEGO Group
## AI is exposing—and reshaping—how work actually happens
AI doesn’t simply accelerate work. It exposes how the organization already operates—and forces it to evolve.
When decisions are produced at machine speed, long-standing ambiguities—who owns the decision, when to challenge, how to escalate—stop staying contained. They spread across teams, workflows, and customer experiences. 
Employees feel that shift directly. While 65% report having guidelines for AI-enabled work, an equal percentage say those guidelines are already outdated. At the same time, leaders report unclear decision rights and governance that can’t keep pace with how AI is changing work.
Those gaps leave employees to rely on individual judgment at exactly the moments that now matter most.
Those individual decisions don’t stay isolated. They accumulate into patterns—shaping how decisions get made, how risk is handled, and how accountability is understood across the enterprise.
Organizations that are intentional about their human operating model--that combine AI maturity with strong change execution--achieve up to 73% higher revenue growth and an 11% improvement in operating margin compared to peers.
## The human operating model behind AI performance: Permission, practice, proof
The organizations pulling ahead are not just deploying AI—they are redesigning how work happens through three critical elements. 
**Permission, practice, and proof**
**Permission**
### Clarifying authority in AI-enabled decisions
AI shifts where decisions happen. Employees increasingly receive recommendations—often without clear guidance on whether they are expected to act, challenge, or override them.
This ambiguity is widespread. More than one-third of executives report unclear escalation paths for risky AI outputs, and 43% say employees do not feel safe raising concerns.
In practice, that uncertainty shapes behavior. When authority is unclear or challenge feels risky, people defer to the system—or wait for others to act.
Leading organizations address this directly by:
  * Defining when AI should be followed, questioned, or overridden
  * Making escalation paths explicit and usable
  * Assigning clear ownership when human judgment and AI outputs diverge


Over time, these choices reduce hesitation and create more consistent decision-making across workflows.
**Practice**
### Making judgment repeatable
Even with clear authority, organizations face a second challenge: consistency. When AI outputs are incomplete, uncertain, or wrong, the outcome depends on how employees interpret and act. 
Today, that capability is uneven. Only 27% of employees say managers primarily focus on coaching judgment and strategic interpretation—yet that role is expected to expand rapidly as AI becomes embedded in daily work. 
At the same time, performance is becoming harder to evaluate. Ninety-three percent of executives say AI-enabled work makes it significantly more difficult to assess contribution and accountability.
Organizations that close this gap treat judgment as something that can be built, not assumed. They:
  * Codify examples of strong decision-making
  * Embed checkpoints into workflows
  * Equip managers to coach interpretation, not just execution 


As these routines take hold, decision quality becomes more consistent—and less dependent on individual discretion.
**Proof**
### Reinforcing the behaviors that matter
The final lever is reinforcement. Employees learn what matters by observing what gets rewarded, recognized, and promoted. 
In many organizations, the signal is still skewed toward speed and adoption. While 81% of executives say their organizations reward AI-related skills, fewer recognize employees who appropriately question or challenge AI outputs. 
That imbalance shapes behavior. When speed is rewarded more visibly than discernment, employees optimize for throughput—even when the risks are higher.
Organizations that lead in AI take a different approach:
  * Redesigning performance management systems for human-AI work
  * Rewarding escalation and responsible intervention
  * Reinforcing accountability in human–AI decisions


These signals accumulate. Over time, they determine whether employees rely on AI blindly—or engage with it thoughtfully.
> “If we apply AI inside each silo only to improve individual work efficiency, the benefit disappears at the enterprise level. To operate AI enterprise‑wide, enterprise‑wide reform is necessary.” 
> **Yasuo Kawai** Vice President, Head of Digital Unit, Honda Motor Company
## From adoption to advantage
Most organizations are still focused on adoption—deploying AI tools and scaling use cases. But adoption alone does not translate into enterprise performance.
Without redesigning workflows, clarifying decision-making, and reinforcing the right behaviors, AI gains remain localized—improving individual tasks without strengthening the enterprise as a whole.
Organizations pulling ahead take a broader view. They:
  * Redesign work across end-to-end workflows
  * Embed AI into cross-functional processes
  * Align decision rights, routines, and incentives with how work is actually done


The result is not just faster execution—but more consistent, scalable outcomes.
## What this means for leaders
For strategy, operations, and people leaders, the priority is not simply implementing AI. It is redesigning how the organization operates with it.
Three actions stand out:
  * **Clarify decision authority** Define where human judgment is required, how escalation works, and who owns decisions in AI-enabled workflows.
  * **Build judgment into everyday work** Establish routines, training, and managerial practices that make strong decision-making consistent.
  * **Align reinforcement with outcomes** Reward behaviors that reflect quality, accountability, and appropriate challenge—not just speed and adoption.


These choices shape how work gets done day to day—and determine how reliably the organization can translate AI into results.
## The real source of AI value
AI is often framed as a technology investment. In practice, it is a redesign of how decisions are made, how work flows, and how accountability is enforced.
Organizations seeing the strongest results are not simply deploying advanced models. They are rethinking the system around them—how decisions are structured, how judgment is applied, and how outcomes are reinforced.
That is what turns isolated gains into sustained performance.
The question for leaders is no longer whether to adopt AI. It is whether the organization is designed to absorb it—and translate it into meaningful advantage. 
**FAQs**
How are organizations defining decision rights for AI?
Many organizations haven’t fully defined them. Seven in 10 executives report unclear decision rights in AI-enabled work, even as nearly two-thirds say AI is already reshaping roles and workflows. 
Where decision rights are clearer, organizations tend to specify when AI outputs are followed, when human judgment is required, and who holds override authority—reducing the need for employees to interpret those boundaries on their own. 
How are organizational and business leaders clarifying accountability in AI workflows?
Accountability is becoming harder to track as AI becomes embedded in work. Ninety-three percent of executives say AI-enabled work makes performance more difficult to evaluate, and 50% of employees say it’s harder to receive fair credit. 
Organizations with clearer accountability make ownership more visible—especially when human judgment and AI outputs diverge—so responsibility doesn’t become diffused. 
How are enterprises governing AI-driven decisions effectively?
In many cases, governance isn’t keeping pace with adoption. While 64% of employees report having AI guidelines, an equal percentage say those guidelines are already outdated. Seventy-seven percent of technology leaders say AI adoption is moving faster than governance capabilities. 
Where governance is more effective, it shows up within workflows themselves—through clearer escalation paths, defined checkpoints, and more explicit decision authority at the point of action. 
How is decision authority shifting in AI-enabled work?
AI is already changing where decisions are made. Sixty-three percent of executives say AI has shifted who makes key decisions, and 70% report that unclear authority has led to issues in AI projects. 
As more recommendations are generated upstream, decisions increasingly move closer to the edge of the organization—making clarity around authority and escalation more consequential. 
How is decision-making evolving in AI-enabled organizations?
Most organizations are still in transition. Only 15% combine advanced AI maturity with strong change execution, yet those that do report up to 73% higher revenue growth and an 11% improvement in operating margin. 
The difference shows up in how decisions are structured—how AI inputs, human judgment, and checkpoints are integrated across workflows, rather than handled in isolation.
