**Executive summary**
Board members at energy companies have started asking questions about AI — but leaders might not have enough answers. The key issue is governance, for the investments, risks, and other areas tied to the rapid expansion of AI solutions across the industry. Companies need to show boards that they have moved beyond disconnected AI experiments, to an established process that identifies and prioritizes a catalog of potential AI use cases so that they can drive success and deliver business value that aligns with enterprise goals.

## Boards want to know
Energy companies are investing in AI solutions, but many of their boards are unclear about how and why those investments have been made.
“From a governance perspective, board members want to know how and why you made decisions, and how you will fuel revenue, contain costs, ensure compliance and mitigate risks.”
[ Jonathan Eaton  ](https://www.grantthornton.com/people/north-carolina/charlotte/eaton-jonathan)
Advisory Services Partner Grant Thornton Advisors LLC 
Board members might support AI initiatives but, as energy firms scale AI [**beyond small efficiencies**](https://www.grantthornton.com/insights/articles/energy/2025/ai-can-illuminate-strategies-in-energy) to enterprise-scale solutions, boards will ask more questions. “The thing that’s paramount to keep in mind is that the board’s role is to govern,” said Grant Thornton Business Consulting Principal Jonathan Eaton. “From a governance perspective, board members want to know how and why you made decisions, and how you will fuel revenue, contain costs, ensure compliance and mitigate risks.”
[**Grant Thornton’s 2026 AI Impact Survey**](https://www.grantthornton.com/insights/survey-reports/energy/2026/energy-insights-2026-ai-impact-survey) found that 64% of energy boards have integrated AI risk into ongoing oversight, nine percentage points above the cross-industry average. Yet, only 17% of energy leaders say their organizations are ready for an independent audit of AI governance today, five percentage points lower than the average across industries.
To demonstrate effective governance, energy leaders need to show that they are managing AI decisions, costs and risks to select and implement the best AI use cases.
Many companies have not taken a structured enterprise approach to early AI initiatives. “Sometimes, department leaders have had money in their budget and elected to test an opportunity in AI,” said Grant Thornton Energy Industry Head Tyler Jones. “But they might not see that money again, and they might not know entirely what came out of it.”
“The C-suite executives we're meeting with are saying that the board has pressed them to avoid letting departments or leaders who have money be the sole factor for what AI projects get funded,” Eaton said. “You have to put structure behind that decision, as a management team.”
“Boards are increasingly asking whether AI is being scaled where it delivers value”, said Vikrant Rai, Managing Director Risk Advisory, Grant Thornton. Many board members will support AI initiatives, if they can see the connection to enterprise value.
Boards want to see that AI governance structures are guiding how and where energy companies allocate their AI investments.

## **AI use case governance**
An energy company needs AI governance that provides a clear structure to evaluate and prioritize AI use cases based on their enterprise value rather than isolated team motives.
The foundation of governance is a catalog of potential use cases. Without that, companies are limited to pursuing the projects that teams advocate, as structured by those teams. With a catalog of use cases, an energy company can identify where and how AI will get the broadest support from teams across the company, and how AI can deliver the most value. The catalog must be:
  * **Centralized** and continuously updated for AI use cases across the enterprise
  * **Comprehensive** of both proposed and active use cases across operations, finance, trading, customer and corporate functions
  * **Standardized** with metadata that captures each case’s business objective, value hypothesis, data dependencies, risk profile, owner and status
  * **Responsive** with explicit links to business pain points and measurable outcomes
  * **Proactive** , with a design that surfaces opportunities rather than relying on departmental advocacy


With this central catalog of use cases, boards can see that leaders are evaluating AI opportunities systematically rather than funding isolated experiments. That structured evaluation can form governance with several elements.
### Prioritization
“Prioritization becomes much clearer when anchored in proven value-based initiatives”, Rai said. Use cases like predictive maintenance are consistently delivering significant returns, and use cases like renewable forecasting can deliver returns even sooner. That helps organizations move from experimentation to disciplined portfolio decisions.
A use case intake and prioritization framework replaces ad hoc funding decisions and creates consistency in how opportunities are evaluated. It should define how ideas enter and move through the use case catalog with:
  * A standard intake workflow for all AI initiatives
  * Clear screening criteria for strategic alignment, value potential, feasibility and risk
  * A formal prioritization model that balances ROI, urgency and enterprise impact
  * Stage gates from concept to pilot to scale


### Strategy
Boards are specifically pushing leadership to demonstrate AI strategic alignment and funding guardrails. This alignment connects use case decisions to the enterprise strategy with:
  * An explicit linkage between use cases and strategic priorities
  * Guardrails to prevent opportunistic or siloed investments
  * Capital allocation aligned to prioritized use case clusters


Boards are also pushing organizations to look beyond core operations. “We’re seeing strong results in back-office AI use cases, for example, with automated regulatory reporting delivering 150–200% ROI with a –four- to eight-month payback, and AI-driven customer operations achieving higher satisfaction levels than traditional models,” Rai said. “That shifts AI from an operational tool to a strategic lever.”
### Consensus
Cross-functional alignment for the governance operating model brings the enterprise together around AI decisions. This reduces fragmentation and ensures consistent decision-making enterprise-wide with:
  * Coordination across business units, IT, data, risk, legal and compliance
  * Governance forums or councils that review the use case portfolio
  * Integration with existing enterprise governance structures


### Decision rights
Formal decision rights are critical to avoid fragmented ownership and inconsistent outcomes. A decision rights and accountability structure clarifies who owns AI decisions at every stage with:
  * A defined RACI across business, technology, risk and compliance
  * Clear ownership for each use case in the catalog
  * Escalation pathways for high-risk or high-investment decisions
  * Board visibility into major approvals and exceptions


### Dependencies
Weak data foundations are a frequent cause of underperforming AI initiatives. Data foundation and dependency governance helps to ensure use cases are viable and reliable with:
  * Clear data ownership, quality standards and integration requirements
  * Validation checkpoints before use case approval
  * Ongoing reconciliation and data governance controls


### Risk tiering
A risk-tiering model aligns governance effort to use case risk exposure, maintaining control while allowing scalability. It can help ensure governance is proportionate to risk with:
  * Classifications for each use case based on technical, operational, regulatory and ethical risk
  * Tiering that drives required controls, approvals and monitoring intensity
  * Mandates for deeper scrutiny and more formal oversight for high-risk use cases


### Controls
An integrated risk, compliance and controls framework helps ensure that governance spans design, deployment and ongoing operation. It should embed governance across the lifecycle with:
  * Security, privacy and third-party risk reviews embedded in intake and approval
  * Alignment with regulatory frameworks and internal policies
  * Controls tailored to each risk tier


### Measurement
Value measurement and portfolio management address the common gap where AI experimentation outpaces measurable results. This element should show whether investments are delivering outcomes, with:
  * Defined KPIs for each use case, such as financial impact, efficiency gains or risk reduction
  * A portfolio-level view of AI value creation
  * Mechanisms to stop or reallocate underperforming initiatives


“One practical approach we use with clients is benchmarking performance against sector baselines,” Rai said. “With energy AI averaging roughly 170% ROI, anything materially below that threshold becomes a candidate for re-evaluation or reallocation at the portfolio level.”
### Monitoring
“Monitoring isn’t just about model drift, it’s about value drift. A use case that initially performs well but trends downward over time should trigger the same governance response as a control failure.”
[ Vikrant Rai  ](https://www.grantthornton.com/people/new-jersey/edison/rai-vikrant)
Partner, Cyber & Risk Advisory Grant Thornton Advisors LLC 
“Monitoring isn’t just about model drift, it’s about value drift”, Rai said. “A use case that initially performs well but trends downward over time should trigger the same governance response as a control failure.”
Boards want evidence that controls are operating effectively over time. A plan for monitoring, auditability and performance oversight helps maintain trust after deployment with:
  * Continuous monitoring for model performance, drift and anomalies
  * Audit trails for decisions, approvals and changes
  * Regular review cadences with reporting to leadership and the board


### AI inventory
An AI system inventory provides traceability that connects governance to execution. Boards and auditors increasingly expect an inventory with traceability that includes:
  * An extension of the use case catalog into a full AI system registry
  * Links that trace each AI system back to its originating use case and approval record
  * Tracking for models, data sources, vendors and deployment status


### Governance that ensures and demonstrates
When AI governance is structured, implemented, and presented effectively, it can help ensure and demonstrate that:
  * Leaders have a comprehensive view of AI opportunities through the use case catalog
  * Investment is guided by strategy rather than individual advocacy
  * Decision-making authority is clear and accountable
  * Initiative evaluation is consistent for value and risk
  * Controls are scaling with risk and are continuously monitored


This directly addresses a board’s concern that AI investments could become fragmented or opportunistic. It shows that the company is proactively finding and evaluating the best enterprise opportunities for AI rather than reactively choosing only among those that internal teams advocate from their areas.
Most of all, this governance shows that the company has positioned AI as a governed portfolio of value creation. At the core of that portfolio is the company’s catalog of potential AI use cases.

## **AI use cases**
“Now, boards are asking how you deploy AI in the back office to improve the time to close, or accelerate robust, accurate and transparent financial information.”
[ Tyler Jones  ](https://www.grantthornton.com/people/texas/dallas/jones-tyler)
Head of Energy Industry Grant Thornton Advisors LLC Partner, Audit Services, Grant Thornton LLP 
A catalog of relevant use cases not only demonstrates that you understand the potential applications of AI — it also helps you align for success.
“I think that your best bet at implementing and monetizing the use of AI is at that intersection of AI technology and a real need where you’ve been struggling,” Eaton said. Jones added, “A lot of use cases have involved asset utilization, optimization and the predictive maintenance side. For energy companies, that was the forefront. Now, boards are asking how you deploy AI in the back office to improve the time to close, or accelerate robust, accurate and transparent financial information.”
“Energy companies are starting to brainstorm about how to deploy AI in accounting and finance functions,” Jones said. “That's going to manifest itself in the financial statements of the company, so the accuracy and relevance of that information become very important to the board.” Preliminary use cases in the energy industry can address needs for specific business profiles.
“A structured use case catalog should reflect both breadth and proven value”, Rai said. “Across the energy sector, we see more than 20 high-impact use cases spanning grid, assets, trading, customer and ESG, with leading examples like predictive maintenance, renewable forecasting and outage optimization consistently delivering measurable ROI and operational resilience.” Even with relatively modest adoption levels, the concentration of value in a handful of use cases is what allows organizations to treat AI as a portfolio of value creation rather than isolated pilots.
1. Margin visibility and leakage detection
**Energy company profile**
Energy companies with complex revenue streams, multiple contract structures or significant commodity cost variability can be at risk of margin leakage. Integrated utilities, midstream companies managing throughput-based fees and energy retailers balancing wholesale procurement against retail pricing can lose margin across tolling agreements, transportation tariffs, fuel procurement or customer rate classes. Their margin analysis can be slow and incomplete due to data that is fragmented across commodity trading systems, SCADA platforms, billing systems and regulatory filings.
**Business value**
AI-driven margin analytics give finance leaders a unified, real-time view of where margin is earned and lost across products, customers and channels. When leaders can identify leakage sources in pricing, procurement, production or the product mix, they can apply targeted interventions to improve gross margin without requiring top-line revenue growth.
**How it works**
AI-driven margin analytics unify operational, financial and market data into a single source of truth. Variance analysis and root-cause diagnostic models identify where margins erode, why it happens and which factors are most likely to drive future change. Predictive costing models support faster and more confident pricing decisions.
**Risks and governance**
AI models can have inaccurate cost allocation logic embedded, which produces misleading margin views that drive suboptimal strategic decisions. Data integration failures across ERP, production and commercial systems can also create blind spots. Overreliance on AI-generated margin insights without validating their underlying assumptions can lead to misguided pricing or portfolio decisions. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Validate the cost allocation methodology with finance and operations leadership before deployment.
  * Establish data reconciliation controls between source systems and the analytics platform.
  * Require CFO-level review of margin insights before strategic decisions are made.
  * Document model assumptions and update them when accounting policies or cost structures change.


**How to get results**
This is a finance-led use case that requires close collaboration among the CFO, operations and commercial leadership. Building a unified data foundation is a prerequisite, and teams must be prepared to act on insights rather than simply review dashboards. Sustained margin improvement requires ongoing model refinement and governance.
2. Dynamic pricing optimization
**Energy company profile**
Energy retailers in deregulated markets, and midstream companies negotiating capacity and transportation rates across multiple counterparties, can struggle with static pricing models. These models are often based on historical cost recovery, leaving revenue on the table during high-demand periods. Pricing adjustments are often slow and disconnected from real-time commodity movements, weather-driven demand shifts and competitor offers.
**Business value**
AI-driven pricing enables companies to respond to demand shifts, input cost changes and competitive moves faster than traditional pricing processes allow. Capturing price in favorable market conditions, and defending margin in downturns, generate revenue and margin improvements that static, cost-plus pricing models consistently miss.
**How it works**
In these solutions, AI models analyze demand signals, competitor pricing, customer purchasing behavior and input cost trends to recommend pricing adjustments in near-real time. Reinforcement learning and predictive analytics are the primary techniques involved.
**Risks and governance**
If algorithms are poorly governed, they can trigger pricing changes that risk customer relationships, channel conflict or regulatory scrutiny, especially if pricing patterns appear discriminatory or collusive. Models that are trained on historical data might behave unpredictably in uncommon market conditions. Human review protocols are important to help ensure any erroneous price recommendations are not executed at scale. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Define minimum and maximum pricing boundaries that AI recommendations cannot breach without human approval.
  * Establish customer segment and channel-specific pricing governance policies.
  * Require legal and compliance review of algorithm design for regulatory risk.
  * Monitor pricing outcomes against margin and customer retention targets and review model logic quarterly.


**How to get results**
Dynamic pricing represents a significant cultural shift for teams that are accustomed to stable price lists. Sales teams need training and confidence in AI-generated recommendations, and finance must establish governance frameworks that define acceptable pricing boundaries and approval thresholds.
3. Predictive maintenance
**Energy company profile**
Asset-intensive energy companies where unplanned downtime carries cost and safety risk stand to benefit most. Midstream operators managing pipelines and compressor stations, upstream producers with drilling and wellhead equipment and utilities operating generation and distribution infrastructure can have reactive maintenance models that are expensive. Unplanned outages can cascade into contractual penalties and regulatory consequences.
**Business value**
Predictive maintenance reduces unplanned downtime and extends asset life by identifying failure patterns before they cause disruption. Companies that shift from reactive to condition-based maintenance models can achieve significant reductions in maintenance costs and production losses, with direct improvements to asset utilization and operating margin.
**How it works**
Successful predictive maintenance solutions apply AI machine learning models that are trained on the sensor and IoT data to identify patterns that precede equipment failure. They apply condition monitoring, anomaly detection and failure prediction models to help maintenance teams intervene before a breakdown occurs.
**Risks and governance**
Poorly trained AI models can generate false positives that erode maintenance team confidence, and false negatives that allow failures to occur. Sensor data gaps, integration failures between operational technology and IT systems and insufficient model retraining cycles can all reduce reliability and increase costs rather than reduce them. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Establish model performance thresholds and alert protocols.
  * Define human review requirements for all high-stakes maintenance decisions.
  * Implement regular model retraining schedules tied to new failure data.
  * Enable continuous performance evaluation and accountability by maintaining audit trails that link AI recommendations to maintenance actions and outcomes.


**How to get results**
To effectively adopt predictive maintenance, businesses need to rethink maintenance scheduling, workforce roles and the relationship between operations and finance. Maintenance teams need to shift from calendar-based to condition-based workflows, and finance must build new models for capital planning that reflect reduced unplanned costs over time. On the technical side, solutions typically require AI strategy, data architecture and change management support. 
4. Demand and production planning
**Energy company profile**
Utilities, power generators and natural gas distributors often have planning teams that rely on historical load curves and manual adjustments. These tools cannot keep pace with demand patterns sensitive to weather, seasonal cycles or industrial customer variability. Forecast errors drive costly overgeneration or expensive spot market purchases. 
**Business value**
[**AI-enhanced forecasting for demand and production planning**](https://www.grantthornton.com/insights/case-studies/energy/2025/dynamic-agility-midstream-energy) can reduce forecast error, improve inventory positioning and align production schedules more precisely with actual demand. For finance leaders, this improved planning accuracy translates to lower working capital requirements, reduced expediting costs and more reliable revenue recognition. These are measurable benefits that appear quickly on the balance sheet.
**How it works**
Machine learning enhances forecast accuracy by identifying correlations across financial, operational and external data, including market signals, weather patterns and buying trends. Human planners cannot process that data at scale as effectively. Natural language interfaces allow finance and operations users to query forecasts without data engineering support. 
**Risks and governance**
Overreliance on AI forecasts without human review can amplify errors during demand shocks or market disruptions that fall outside the model’s training data. Poor data quality from ERP or sales systems can produce unreliable outputs, and a lack of transparency in model logic can make it difficult to identify and correct errors. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Require human review of AI forecasts before production commitments are made.
  * Establish data quality standards, with monitoring for all input data sources.
  * Define escalation protocols for forecast anomalies.
  * Conduct regular back-testing of model accuracy and document forecast overrides with rationale to support continuous model improvement.


**How to get results**
Finance leaders play a central role in this use case, because improved forecast accuracy directly affects working capital requirements, inventory valuation and revenue recognition timing. To ensure successful adoption, businesses must integrate AI outputs into existing planning systems and establish new cross-functional rhythms between finance, sales and operations.
5. Inventory and working capital optimization
**Energy company profile**
Utilities, midstream operators and energy services companies might manage large spare parts inventories for critical infrastructure, including specialized or long-lead-time components. Excess inventory ties up working capital, while insufficient inventory extends outage durations and inflates emergency procurement costs. Most companies manage this trade-off manually or with rigid rules-based systems.
**Business value**
AI-driven inventory management reduces excess stock, minimizes obsolescence risk and improves service levels simultaneously — a combination that rules-based systems rarely achieve. For CFOs, the primary benefit is a measurable working capital reduction, with secondary benefits including lower warehousing costs and improved cash conversion cycle performance.
**How it works**
These solutions use machine learning to predict demand at the SKU level, forecast replenishment timing and identify obsolescence risk before it materializes. Computer vision and RFID integration can add real-time location and condition data to inventory models.
**Risks and governance**
Overly aggressive inventory reduction recommendations can create service failures and lost sales if demand spikes or supplier lead times extend unexpectedly. Models that do not account for supply chain disruption risk might optimize for normal conditions while leaving the business exposed during periods of volatility or supplier stress. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Set safety stock floors that AI recommendations cannot override without supply chain leadership approval.
  * Establish service-level targets as hard constraints in optimization models.
  * Monitor AI-driven inventory decisions against actual service outcomes monthly.
  * Require human review before acting on recommendations that deviate significantly from established reorder policies.


**How to get results**
For CFOs, inventory optimization is one of the clearest paths to measurable working capital improvement from AI. To succeed, these solutions can require organizational change that includes redefining reorder policies, adjusting procurement team incentives and integrating AI recommendations into existing ERP workflows.
6. Supply chain risk management
**Energy company profile**
Energy companies with complex vendor networks, sole-source dependencies or geopolitical exposure can have heightened supply chain risks. Renewable energy developers dependent on imported solar panels, turbine components and battery systems face concentrated supplier risk. Utilities and midstream operators relying on specialized transformers, compressors and pipeline materials experience similar exposure. Traditional procurement tools cannot model alternative sourcing scenarios at the needed speed.
**Business value**
Finance and procurement leaders can use continuous AI-driven supplier risk scoring to help identify and respond to supplier vulnerabilities before they disrupt production. This proactive risk management reduces the financial impact of supply chain failures, lowers the costs of expediting and alternative sourcing, and improves supply chain resilience over time.
**How it works**
In these solutions, AI models ingest supplier financial data, news feeds, logistics indicators and geopolitical signals to score supplier risk continuously. Then,  [**scenario modeling tools**](https://www.grantthornton.com/insights/articles/energy/2026/plan-now-for-agility-in-energy) allow finance and procurement teams to evaluate the cost and feasibility of alternative sourcing strategies before a crisis occurs.
**Risks and governance**
AI models that rely on publicly available data might miss private supplier risks that are not yet visible in external signals, including financial distress or operational problems. False risk signals can damage supplier relationships and trigger unnecessary sourcing changes that introduce new costs and quality risks. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Define risk score thresholds that trigger specific response actions and require human validation before supplier relationship changes are made.
  * Establish a supplier communication protocol that ensures AI-generated risk assessments are reviewed and contextualized before being acted upon.
  * Audit model inputs and scoring logic at least annually for accuracy and fairness.


**How to get results**
To successfully implement supply chain AI solutions, companies need a data-sharing framework with key suppliers and a cross-functional governance structure that connects procurement, operations and finance. Finance leaders often drive this initiative, because the financial exposure from supply chain failure is directly visible on the income statement.
7. Procurement cost optimization
**Energy company profile**
Procurement teams often lack the analytical bandwidth to identify savings across contracts for field services, materials, fleet management and facilities maintenance at utilities and midstream companies with large indirect spending categories, complex service contracts and high vendor concentration. Losses from maverick spending, contract compliance failures and missed volume discounts are common.
**Business value**
Companies can use AI analysis of procurement data to find savings opportunities that manual analysis cannot identify at scale, in areas like contract compliance, spend consolidation and supplier negotiation. Midmarket companies that apply AI to indirect spending categories can even find recoverable savings that are several percentage points of their total procurement spending.
**How it works**
These solutions use AI analysis of procurement data to identify patterns of overspending, flag contract noncompliance and surface consolidation opportunities. Natural language processing enables AI to extract key terms from contracts and match them against actual purchase behavior at scale.
**Risks and governance**
If AI-generated savings recommendations are based on incomplete or inaccurate spending data, they can produce misleading results. Automating procurement decisions that do not have sufficient human oversight can create compliance failures, supplier relationship damage or unintended sole-source dependencies. Adoption may be slowed by resistance from procurement teams that perceive AI as a threat to their judgment. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Require human review and approval for all AI-generated contract or supplier changes above defined spend thresholds.
  * Establish data quality standards for procurement data inputs.
  * Define a change management process for implementing savings recommendations.
  * Monitor compliance with AI-recommended procurement policies and report exceptions to finance leadership quarterly.


**How to get results**
This use case requires close alignment among procurement, finance and legal functions. Companies must pair technology enablement with process redesign and supplier relationship management to capture the identified savings. They must also apply governance frameworks to ensure that AI recommendations are reviewed and approved appropriately.
8. Accounts payable and invoice reconciliation
**Energy company profile**
Energy companies often need to process significant invoice volume when they have joint venture operations, multiple entities or complex royalty arrangements. Upstream producers managing joint interest billing, midstream companies processing throughput-based invoices and utilities handling large contractor volumes can benefit as well. Manual processing commonly produces discrepancies, duplicate payments and missed early payment discounts.
**Business value**
In accounts payable and invoice reconciliation, AI matching engines can process invoices faster, with greater accuracy and at a lower cost than manual processes. Straight-through processing rates improve, early payment discount capture increases and duplicate payment risk decreases. For finance leaders, AP automation delivers measurable cost reduction and improved cash management with a relatively rapid time to value.
**How it works**
These solutions use AI matching engines to compare invoices, purchase orders and receipts in real time, flagging exceptions and routing them for human review only when necessary. They apply machine learning to improve matching accuracy over time by learning from exception patterns.
**Risks and governance**
If matching errors go undetected, they can result in overpayments, duplicate payments or missed obligations. If solutions apply over automation without adequate exception handling, they can allow processing of fraudulent invoices that match purchase order data convincingly. System integration failures between AI solutions and ERPs can create reconciliation gaps that are difficult to detect. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Define confidence thresholds below which all matches require human review.
  * Implement fraud detection controls that flag invoices from new or infrequently used vendors.
  * Establish exception reporting and escalation workflows.
  * Conduct periodic audits of straight-through processed invoices to validate matching accuracy.
  * Maintain full audit trails for all automated payment approvals.


**How to get results**
For the finance function, this is often one of the fastest and most measurable AI implementations. The organizational change is relatively contained, primarily affecting the accounts payable team’s workflows and exception management processes. That makes this use case a good starting point for companies new to AI-enabled finance automation.
9. Financial planning, analysis and forecasting
**Energy company profile**
Utilities managing rate case filings, midstream companies forecasting throughput and upstream producers modeling production economics all face forecasting complexity. Organizations exposed to commodity price volatility often have finance teams can spend significant time on manual data aggregation. Rolling forecasts are labor-intensive and quickly become outdated as market conditions shift.
**Business value**
AI capabilities can reduce the time that finance teams spend on data aggregation and report production, which frees up their capacity for higher-value analysis and business partnering. Predictive forecasting models can improve accuracy and enable faster scenario analysis, giving CFOs a more reliable basis for capital allocation decisions and a stronger voice in [**strategic planning conversations**](https://www.grantthornton.com/insights/case-studies/energy/2025/dynamic-agility-midstream-energy). 
**How it works**
These solutions apply generative AI and machine learning to automate data aggregation, reconciliation and narrative generation for financial reports. They use predictive models to improve forecast accuracy by incorporating operational and external data that traditional FP&A tools cannot process. They can even employ well-governed agentic AI to complete multistep analysis workflows with minimal human input.
**Risks and governance**
AI-generated forecasts that finance leaders cannot explain or interrogate can undermine their credibility with boards and auditors. When models are trained only on historical data, they can perform poorly during structural business changes. Generative AI tools that produce narrative commentary might generate plausible-sounding but inaccurate explanations of financial results, which means that companies need to carefully review their outputs. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Require human review and sign-off on all AI-generated forecasts before sharing them with leaders or the board.
  * Establish explainability standards for forecasting models, ensuring that finance leaders can explain key drivers.
  * Implement version control for models and document all methodology changes.
  * Audit AI-generated narratives for factual accuracy before publication.


**How to get results**
When finance leaders adopt AI for FP&A, they must redesign team workflows and redefine the value-added work their analysts perform. The shift from data assembly to strategic analysis requires new skills and a different relationship between finance and the business units it serves. 
10. Financial close acceleration
**Energy company profile**
Financial close can be time-consuming for energy companies with complex consolidations, multiple legal entities or high transaction volumes. It can also be a challenge for utilities with regulated and unregulated subsidiaries, upstream producers with joint venture interests and midstream companies managing intercompany settlements. Manual reconciliation is a persistent bottleneck, particularly with complex intercompany eliminations and layered regulatory reporting requirements.
**Business value**
AI-driven reconciliation and anomaly detection compress close cycles by automating the most time-consuming manual steps. Faster close cycles improve financial reporting timeliness, reduce month-end stress on finance teams and provide leaders with earlier access to financial results. That, in turn, improves the quality and speed of management decision-making throughout the organization.
**How it works**
These solutions use AI to automate reconciliation tasks, identify anomalies in account balances and generate draft explanations for variances that require management review. Natural language processing generates narrative commentary for management reports automatically.
**Risks and governance**
If human review protocols are insufficient, automated reconciliations can contain errors that go undetected. If AI-generated variance explanations are factually incorrect, they can mislead management and create audit risk. Overconfidence in AI-assisted close processes can lead finance teams to reduce their review rigor in ways that allow material errors to go undetected. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Define materiality thresholds below which automated reconciliations are accepted and above which human review is required.
  * Establish a mandatory review protocol for all AI-generated variance commentary.
  * Maintain complete audit trails for every automated close step.
  * Ensure external auditors are briefed on AI-assisted close processes and that audit evidence requirements are fully met.


**How to get results**
To accelerate financial close with AI, companies must redesign close workflows, establish clear human review protocols for AI-generated outputs and build auditability into every automated step. Finance leaders must also establish governance for how to review and sign off on AI-generated reconciliations.
11. Quality control and defect detection
**Energy company profile**
Manual inspection of pipelines, welds and components can be too slow and inconsistent at scale. Pipeline operators, refinery operators and renewable energy companies inspecting turbine blades and solar panels have critical infrastructure integrity requirements and significant safety exposure. Undetected defects can create safety incidents, regulatory penalties and costly outages.
**Business value**
Visual inspection that is AI-powered can detect defects at production-line speeds with greater consistency than human inspectors. That can reduce the rates for rework, scrap and warranty claims. For finance leaders, the measurable outcomes include lower cost of quality, reduced warranty reserves and improved customer satisfaction scores that support increased pricing power and contract retention.
**How it works**
These solutions apply computer vision models trained on images of acceptable and defective products, inspecting at production-line speeds with greater consistency than human inspectors. Predictive analytics models identify process conditions associated with higher defect rates before defects occur.
**Risks and governance**
Computer vision models that are trained on limited defect samples might miss novel defect types that are not represented in the training data. If model performance degrades and does not have sufficient human oversight, an overreliance on AI inspection can allow defects to escape detection at scale. Misclassification of acceptable parts as defective items can generate unnecessary scrap and production delays. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Establish minimum model accuracy thresholds before production deployment, for both defect detection and false positive rates.
  * Implement ongoing monitoring of model performance against human inspector validation samples.
  * Define protocols for model retraining when new defect types are identified.
  * Maintain records of all AI inspection decisions, to support warranty and regulatory audit requirements.


**How to get results**
To deploy AI-driven quality control, companies need to integrate production line systems, team training and new metrics for measuring inspection performance. When companies do this well, finance leaders can benefit from quantifying the reduction in warranty costs and scrap rates that the investment generates
12. Process mining and bottleneck identification
**Energy company profile**
Energy companies with complex operational or administrative processes that have not been systematically analyzed for efficiency can have hidden inefficiencies. Utilities managing interconnection queues and outage restoration, midstream companies with nomination and scheduling workflows and upstream producers handling permitting and well completion processes can accumulate inefficiencies that consume significant time and cost when examined in detail.
**Business value**
Process mining can uncover operational and administrative inefficiencies for the first time, giving finance leaders an objective, data-driven basis for decisions about improvement investments. By quantifying the cost of bottlenecks and process deviations, leaders can prioritize improvement initiatives according to financial impact rather than operational intuition or political influence.
**How it works**
Process mining tools use AI to reconstruct actual process flows from system event logs, making bottlenecks and deviations visible for the first time. Digital process automation then eliminates the most costly inefficiencies identified through mining.
**Risks and governance**
The outputs of process mining typically reflect processes as recorded in system logs, not necessarily as they actually occur — so, workarounds and manual interventions that happen outside systems are invisible to the tool. If leaders act on incomplete process maps, improvement investments can address symptoms rather than root causes, wasting capital. Some options to mitigate risk and improve business value:
  * Validate process mining outputs against direct observation and stakeholder input before drawing conclusions.
  * Establish a structured governance process for prioritizing and approving improvement initiatives identified through process mining.
  * Require post-implementation reviews of process changes to validate that targeted improvements were realized and that unintended consequences were identified and addressed.


**How to get results**
This use case often reveals improvement opportunities that cut across functional boundaries, so it requires coordination among operations, finance and IT. Change management is critical, as process changes affect established workflows and individual roles.

## **The ability to solve**
Even if an energy company evaluates a catalog of use cases, new ones will constantly evolve.
In energy, the turbulence of prices, regulations, weather and other factors means that the sheer ability to solve new challenges is a competitive advantage.

“That’s where you could use AI technology to protect revenue and de-risk from a lawsuit perspective,” Eaton said. “Look at how AI can help you track and trace suppliers. Customers are saying they want tax credits, so now you need to certify what you produce.”

“You have to be able to say, in your industry and your subsector, that these are the use cases — this is how value is created and lost, because we know that,” Eaton said. “If you want to create value, protect value or de-risk, here are the choices, here's the prioritization, and here's how they move the needle.” Upstream, downstream, renewable and other sectors can have different goals and solutions. “What are you doing with AI?
