Development communications practitioners are often asked: what’s the real return on investing in policy engagement events? The answer is simple: without the events, research risks becoming a report on a shelf.Done well, event communication is a **trust-building exercise**. It's the one point of a programme where months of work are put to real test under lively discussions in a room full of people who can choose to champion your efforts or quietly file it away. As we plan events in phases, we must think of policy engagement the same way. There is a beginning, a middle, and an end. And we must be working on this trust building exercise from the beginning. 
Just as we advise across all our communication work, event communications should not be an afterthought or a mere dissemination task to tick a box. It should be a strategic exercise to engage, reflect, and amplify your work. 
#### The beginning: build ownership before you build the agenda 
The beginning matters most. If stakeholders are not part of the design, they will not feel ownership of the outcome. In one recent [grant exercise](https://povertyevidence.org/challenge-fund/), we invited academics, policymakers, and practitioners to help shape the research questions before the call was even announced. That co-creation built belonging and legitimacy, because it wasn’t a ‘for your information’ approach, but a ‘could we think together?’ one. For funders, this is **Trust-Building 101:** invest early in dialogue, and you multiply the possibility of impact. This isn’t a new idea. As Caroline Cassidy put it back in 2013, if you’re not talking and listening to your audience from the beginning, what right do you have to assume anyone is going to listen to you when you have something to say – you’ll be addressing an empty room. ([Is it time to ban the term ‘dissemination’?](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/impactofsocialsciences/2013/08/19/is-it-time-to-ban-the-term-dissemination/) LSE, 2013).Audience must be engaged via multiple channels and by showing up in the spaces they already frequent. We shouldn’t just organise but also participate. Webinars, dialogues, presentations – all are useful tools to show up and earn trust for your mission. 
#### The middle: when trust gets tested in the room
This is the moment where the groundwork from phase one pays off, the moment when funders’ investment in dialogue becomes visible, as policymakers walk into the room, evidence is debated, and trust is tested in real time. Running an event with government stakeholders isn’t just about logistics. It is about legitimacy. Protocols matter: the right seating, the briefing packets, the respect for hierarchy. These details may seem ceremonial, but they signal whether you are seen as a partner or an outsider. 
Equally important is the atmosphere. A stiff, formal event risks becoming a monologue. A lively event with structured Q&As, panel discussions, and honest insights builds momentum. It is here that funders can see the difference between passive attendance and active engagement.And because policy engagement is never risk-free, the middle is also about resilience. Last-minute agenda changes, substitutions of senior officials, or even technical failures are inevitable. What matters is the ability to adapt without losing the thread of dialogue. The guiding question leading our every move, every piece of communication: why should they care?
####  The follow-up: here the relationship either compounds or evaporates 
We’d resist calling this phase an “end” because the real goal is continuity, a trusted relationship that lasts beyond the event itself. Follow-up is not just about sending recap emails or posting photos online; it’s keeping the conversation alive with clear takeaways and credible “what next” steps. Equally, it is about listening: are stakeholders responding, requesting further meetings, or referring to your work in policy discussions? That is the true ROI: when engagement translates into uptake, association, or commitments.Events are rare spaces where development actors, government, and communities share meaning in the same room. But the story doesn’t end when the chairs are stacked. Post-event communication is where funders can see whether dialogue has matured into partnership. 
At this stage, the KPI is the strength of your relationship. Are stakeholders proactively interacting with your team? Do they see you as a trusted partner rather than a one-off organiser? Has your work added any value to their agenda? Diagnostics and review are essential in asking what shifted, what can be replicated, and what must be adapted for different contexts. The underrated truth is that the communication side of events is as critical as the content. A shared plan, a consistent narrative, attention to detail from slide branding to protocol notes; these are signals of professionalism and respect, that build the foundation for sustained trust.
So, **what should funders take away from this?** We must treat events not as a single moment of dissemination, but as a three-phase trust-building process. One that starts well before the room is booked and continues long after it empties. This is where investments in dialogue prove their worth by building networks that endure, shaping whether evidence goes on to influence decisions.
