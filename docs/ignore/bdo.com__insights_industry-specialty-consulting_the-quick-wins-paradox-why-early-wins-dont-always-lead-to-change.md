## Introducing LIFTS and the Operating Discipline That Lets Quick Wins Stick 
Nonprofits face a recurring challenge: How can the organization turn quick wins into sustainable gains? While the answer to that question will vary based on an organization’s goals, there are common pressures that often appear during business transformations. Leadership looking for visible momentum, sponsors seeking proof of progress, and employees assessing the impact they’re making all present tangible reasons for that pressure.
In response, nonprofits can be caught between the need to show meaningful progress in the early stages of a project and the risk that what gets defined as progress doesn’t translate into transformation that sticks. This can lead to leadership looking for wins that may look impressive on paper but lack the proper support structure to become sustained change. 
The issue becomes the quick wins paradox: An organization looks to visible, achievable actions, completes them, and defines them as wins. Then, because the action lacks the necessary support and disciple structure, the momentum that was supposed to compound and become a long-term transformation leaks away instead. 
## Why Do Early Wins Fail To Stick?
Although an organization may have a desire and willingness to put in the effort, projects can falter and ultimately fail without the right approach. This includes building a comprehensive roadmap containing the change network, champions, training, and communications that turn an announcement into a behavior.
These are the visible elements that showcase progress to key stakeholders, but by the sequence of work, aren’t on display during a transformation’s early stages. But the demand for visible progress doesn’t wait. In response, organizations may be tempted to improvise, such as by creating a new dashboard or rewriting a policy — none of which address the heart of the matter.
Motion and progress are not the same, and nonprofits must understand that improvising without structure isn’t the same as taking well-considered transformation steps.
## What Does a Real Quick Win Look Like?
Recognizing the difference between a true quick win and something that’s unstructured requires a clear definition. The LIFTS approach addresses the issue head-on. LIFTS stands for Lightweight, Implementation-bounded, Forward-Targeted Steps. Each word carries weight. 
  * Lightweight means small enough to complete without needing the full suite of change mechanisms to be in place.
  * Implementation-bounded means it can be finished and sustained with the capacity an organization already has, rather than left as a fragment of a deferred implementation that needs the entire organization to hold it up.
  * Forward-Targeted means it points at the destination on the roadmap, not at whatever happens to be easy and visible.
  * Steps means deliberate moves in sequence, not through improvisation.


This approach stands in contrast with quick wins that lack lasting value. When the idea of “quick wins” rewards speed above all else, it invites cutting corners. LIFTS changes that by rewarding bounded, directional progress instead. It emphasizes the concept of performing lightweight — but substantive — work that moves a project forward incrementally.
## What Holds a Quick Win in Place?
That still leaves the issue of the quick wins paradox. If the resources necessary for a project to gain traction aren’t fully in place, what makes a step in the LIFTS method stick? The answer is a lightweight, temporary support, sized to that one action: a Change Scaffold. A Change Scaffold is the minimum adoption support a specific step needs to hold until the required resources and teams are available to make that step permanent.
In practice, a Change Scaffold can be modest: a named owner for an action, a feedback loop to catch anything that breaks, a short note that explains why the change matters, and a check-in to confirm that it held.
This draws a clean line between two grades of change work. Plan-grade change management is the full apparatus necessary for transformation at work, scoped for the entire project and fully realized at the project’s conclusion.
Scaffold-grade is the LIFTS-sized support that makes one early action stick now. The two stay in balance through a simple division of labor: Scaffold design sits in change-management scope; scaffold activation sits in a single LIFTS scope. The change-management workstream designs the minimal support each LIFTS needs; the specific step itself carries that support as it ships. This allows an early win to hold even while the larger change apparatus is not yet live.
## How Can Nonprofits Turn Quick Wins Into Long-Term Success?
Taking incremental progress and turning it into a long-term gain shouldn’t be a compromise. Each step in the LIFTS methodology serves long-term progress through lightweight, achievable progress , with the forward-targeted test as the filter. If an action moves the organization toward where the strategy is going, it qualifies as something worth pursuing; however, if an action only looks like progress but doesn’t move the needle forward, the organization should reconsider before acting.
Filtering this way helps nonprofits steer away from actions that are tempting but don’t deliver sustainable progress, such as:
  * Moves that sit off the project roadmap
  * “Wins” that the organization would later have to undo because they don’t align with organization’s transformation goals
  * Steps that gain stakeholders’ attention but lack the support mechanisms necessary to stick


For example, standing up one shared intake form that the future operating model will keep is a sustainable step that fits the LIFTS approach. But launching a flashy portal that the eventual platform will replace doesn’t qualify, even if it demos well. Declining actions that only appear productive isn’t caution — it’s cutting out distractions to compound incremental progress and represents how a well-run transformation uses those actions to succeed.

## How Should Quick Wins Be Built Into the Plan?
The critical element to understand is that none of this works as improvisation. LIFTS and their scaffolds should be named in a project’s plan from the very beginning, including:
  * Which actions qualify as LIFTS
  * How each step is scaffolded
  * How each ties back to the roadmap


Governed that way, quick wins become a managed asset rather than the ad hoc scope creep they can otherwise become. It also gives stakeholders something that goes beyond a list of completed tasks, providing a short, defensible account of why the organization took each action and how it moves the plan forward.
This process ultimately treats quick-win governance as a named deliverable and serves as an important discipline that can influence whether a strategy is adopted.
