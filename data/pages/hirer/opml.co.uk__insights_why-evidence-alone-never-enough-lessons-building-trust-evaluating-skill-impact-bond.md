##  Authors
  * [Divya Nambiar](https://www.opml.co.uk/person/divya-nambiar "Go to Divya Nambiar's profile") Youth, Skills, and Livelihoods Lead and Principal Consultant
  * [Phalasha Nagpal](https://www.opml.co.uk/search?author=%22Phalasha%20Nagpal%20%28561%29%22 "View all work by Phalasha Nagpal") Former Livelihoods and Social Inclusion Lead and Consultant


When we were selected as the monitoring, evaluation, and learning (MEL) [partner for the Skill Impact Bond in India](https://www.opml.co.uk/projects/assessing-the-impact-of-skill-impact-bond-in-india), we knew the evidence challenge ahead would be significant. What we did not fully anticipate, however, was that the harder challenge would not be generating the evidence, but rather convincing people to believe in it.
The [Skill Impact Bond](https://nsdcindia.org/sib) is the largest development impact bond in the world focused on skills and employment. It targets 50,000 youth and women across India, with an ambitious goal: to redefine what ‘success’ in skills training actually means. Not simply how many young people enrol in a programme, but how many successfully complete their training and get certified, get placed in a job, and – crucially – remain in that job three months later. In a country where placement rates for large-scale skills programmes routinely fall below 20%, and where only one in 10 women who enter skills training are still employed three months on, the stakes are high and the scepticism is real.
Our role was to generate rigorous, concurrent evidence and feed it back into programme design in real time. In practice, this meant following a sample of young men and women from the moment they enrolled in training through to 12 months after they completed it, tracking their journeys through in-depth interviews and exploring why they enrolled in skills training, what their aspirations were, why they stayed in jobs or dropped out, what barriers they faced, and, most crucially, how their experience differed by gender. We ran site visits to 40 manufacturing and service sector workplaces as well as training provider sites across 11 different Indian states. We mapped hundreds of individual journeys to build a picture of what was really happening on the ground, as we have documented in this [report](https://nsdcindia.org/sites/default/files/SkillImpactBondResults_MidTermReport_17072025.pdf).
Walking into rooms full of partners – risk investors, outcome funders, the National Skill Development Corporation, and training providers – we encountered some scepticism about whether we would be able to collect accurate data. Our evaluation methodology came under careful scrutiny from partners with high financial stakes in the project. Partners raised a number of questions. Is it really possible to collect reliable evidence directly from young people on the ground? Would trainees respond to surveys? Would they be able to comprehend the questions and answer them accurately? Could such rigorous data collection be achieved within the rigid timeframes of the project? This kind of data collection, in which we prioritised documenting trainee perspectives, had not been done before at scale in India. Our first task was to win the trust of partners and help them find answers to these questions.
### The task of building trust
One way we approached this was by bringing partners closer to the evidence itself, while maintaining rigorous protocols to ensure data protection and anonymity. A key feature of our evaluation methodology was its trainee-centric approach to data collection, whereby interviews with trainees themselves were used to gather data on whether specific programme outcomes had been achieved. We complemented trainee perspectives with documentary evidence such as offer letters, salary slips, and bank statements. The strength of our evaluation approach lay in its combination of multiple sources of evidence rather than relying on any single method.
Another challenge stemmed from the fact that the project was implemented in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, which required us to conduct telephone interviews with trainees. This raised a number of further questions from partners: Would young people be able to comprehend the questions on certification, placement, and retention? Would trainees (particularly women) answer phone calls and be open to participating in the interviews? How could we ensure that the responses would be authentic, and reflect the perspectives of trainees themselves?
To address these concerns, we designed the questionnaires using clear, plain language from the outset, and then worked with a specialist translation agency to translate them into 11 Indian languages. Each translation was reviewed for simplicity, clarity, and a conversational style to ensure the questions would be easy to understand. To this end, we leveraged our network of staff and colleagues during the pilot to review these tools, ensuring they accounted for regional variations in dialect and used appropriate colloquial terms that would be easy for young people to understand.
In our pilots, we found that trainees were incredibly clear, articulate, and, in fact, eager to share their perspectives. Rather than being a hindrance, we found that the anonymity and distance offered by the telephone interview format also encouraged trainees to speak candidly and share their perspectives openly. At this stage, we were convinced that the tools were ready for use.
Rather than simply state these points ourselves, during one of our training pilot programmes we invited our client to sit in and listen to interview recordings and hear directly from a small, anonymised sample of participants. Listening to trainees speak with clarity about their certification, placement, and three-month retention outcomes helped build their confidence in our data, in a way that reports alone would not have been able to do. This not only helped us build trust but also helped establish our credibility, allowing us to effectively take on the learning partner role early on in the project. With every evaluation cycle, we actively shared the evidence generated, together with the lessons learned, which was then used to make changes to programme implementation.
Nearly four years on, there has been a shift that is rarely captured in formal reporting: partners trust both the evidence and the voices behind it. That trust is what made it possible to engage in more meaningful conversations about what is and isn’t working, and to collectively use the evidence to make course corrections along the way. 
For us, as MEL partners, that has been the real success of the Skill Impact Bond.
### What trust in evidence actually enables
Once that trust had been established, the evidence was able to travel further than we had imagined. The lessons learned were fed directly back into strengthening programme implementation, cohort by cohort.
As part of this work, we developed innovative tools such as the Patthar Patang framework (translated as the Boulder-Kite framework), which maps the barriers young people face in their paths to employment. When you look at [this visual](https://www.opml.co.uk/sites/default/files/2026-07/kite-boulder-framework.pdf), one thing immediately stands out: there are significantly more barriers for women. That evidence did not just sit in a report. We took it back to our partners, including the training providers, in tailored learning workshops and worked with them to adapt their programmes. This also helped the training providers design innovative strategies to recruit new candidates into the programme, and these insights were also used in discussions with employers to consider how to make workplaces more supportive of young women who had often migrated far from home.
At the same time, the Skill Impact Bond’s distributed learning architecture meant that multiple partners could contribute to the learning agenda, each bringing unique insights from their own roles on the project, to strengthen programme outcomes. For example, the National Skill Development Corporation (NSDC) and Dalberg, through the PMU, supported implementation partners in strengthening delivery and embedding monitoring systems that tracked candidate outcomes prior to verification. The British Asian Trust documented lessons learned at the transaction level and synthesised insights from diverse sources to guide models for scaling up the programme. Funders and risk investors engaged closely with the different kinds of evidence that emerged throughout the programme cycle, using this to guide programme decisions and drive continuous improvement.
The results of this approach are clearly visible in the [data](https://nsdcindia.org/sites/default/files/SkillImpactBondResults_MidTermReport_17072025.pdf). In the first cohort, women’s three-month job retention – one of the hardest outcomes to move – stood at 48%. By the fourth cohort, it had risen to 66%. The gap between men and women’s job placement outcomes has also consistently narrowed over time. Against a national backdrop where only around 10% of young women remain in employment three months after training, these figures provide compelling proof of concept for what gender-responsive, outcomes-based financing can achieve.
Those results did not happen because we generated good evidence, however. They happened because partners trusted the evidence enough to drive further improvements on the ground.
### Maximising impact
That credibility has achieved tangible results beyond the programme itself.
Last year, India’s Minister of State for Skill Development and Entrepreneurship launched the Skill Impact Bond results at a major national skills event in New Delhi. The project has also since been cited in India’s Economic Survey, one of the country’s most significant annual economic documents. Earlier this year, that momentum contributed to the launch of the Skills Outcomes Fund, anchored by the NSDC, bringing together government and industry around exactly the kind of outcomes-focused financing model the Skill Impact Bond helped demonstrate. We are now in conversations with the NSDC, whereby we are supporting them to use the evidence generated from our work to brainstorm the design of new kinds of skills training programmes in response to changing workplace requirements.
None of this was in our original terms of reference. It grew from the credibility that patient, rigorous, transparent evidence-building establishes over time.
### What we have learned
The lesson we take from this project is not primarily about methodology, though that matters. It is about something both harder to define and harder to deliver: the sustained relational work of making evidence legible, credible, and usable for our partners who need to act on it.
Many organisations can generate evidence. The differentiator is whether you can sit with partners, adapt how you communicate findings to different audiences, and bring people into the process rather than simply presenting conclusions to them, all while maintaining the rigour that makes the evidence worth trusting in the first place.
In a landscape in which outcomes-based financing is growing, where funders are under pressure to demonstrate impact through reliable evidence, and where the questions facing young people entering the workforce are becoming increasingly complex, the ability to build that trust is not a soft skill: it is the work itself.
