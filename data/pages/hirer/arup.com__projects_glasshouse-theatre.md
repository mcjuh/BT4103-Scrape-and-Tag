The Queensland Government has invested in a new 1,500-seat theatre to expand Queensland’s art and cultural offerings – the Glasshouse Theatre. It is the fifth venue at the Queensland Performing Arts Centre (QPAC), making it Australia’s largest performing arts centre under one roof, and will showcase local, national and international productions, with an estimated 300,000 additional visitors per year.
We collaborated with Blight Rayner Architecture and Snøhetta to develop the international competition-winning design, which incorporated several engineering design initiatives that were pivotal to the win. These included the careful alteration of the existing building to accommodate the new theatre, allowing QPAC to retain two existing dance studios. We worked closely with Lendlease during construction to ensure the inspirational concept of the iconic rippled glazed façade came to life.
Located in the heritage listed Queensland Cultural Centre precinct, the Glasshouse Theatre will meet the demand for greater access to Brisbane venues and provide exceptional performance experiences.
## Engineering a glass façade, cantilevering over busy streets
The inner-city site is extremely tight, with the theatre extending to its border. To provide sufficient circulation space, the theatre foyers cantilever seven metres over the surrounding streets and are punctuated by an undulating glass façade.
This design required careful engineering and an intricate understanding of how materials move together and respond over time. The steel foyer structure suspends four storeys of glass façade over the street, and is supported by a tapered post-tensioned concrete transfer slab – strategically shaped to minimise the amount of concrete required. To ensure the cantilever wouldn’t move during the glass installation, we pre-loaded the roof with weights and validated our approach with regular surveys during installation.

## Designing for a challenging site with risk of flooding
The theatre’s design cleverly responds to the challenges inherent to its location. The site is at risk of flooding, is closely surrounded by five live buildings, must work within an alignment determined by the existing heritage buildings, and is in a constrained position in a popular tourist destination, just metres from The Wheel of Brisbane and South Bank Parklands.
The theatre was designed to withstand a major event comparable to the Brisbane flood event of 2011, while the basement in particular was carefully detailed to maximise flood resilience. Mitigation design measures include an elevated ramp entry, flood barriers at potential entry points, water-retaining concrete, and drained cavities.

## Prefabricated concrete core streamlines construction
We worked closely with Lendlease and its subcontractors to develop a structural system that responded to the site constraints and technical challenges, while being safely constructed. The use of steel and precast concrete panels sped up the build time, minimised disruption to nearby businesses, maximised off-site construction and allowed us to work within the tight site. Collaborating with steel fabricators, Idec Solutions, our team carefully planned the craneage and erection processes.
We also adopted a mixture of temporary and permanent retention systems and open-cut excavation for the basement to reflect the staged construction process. This minimised costs and allowed the existing, surrounding theatres to be used throughout construction, with only one shut-down period.
During construction, we developed a novel approach to building the concrete core – using prefabricated panels that were ‘match cast’ at construction joints. This sped up construction, improved site safety, and eliminated the requirement for space and cost-intensive scaffolding. Although this approach is common in bridge building, this was its first use in Brisbane’s property sector, and it has since become widespread across the city’s construction sites.
## Saving 220 tonnes of embodied carbon
To further improve the theatre’s sustainability and lower its embodied carbon, we adopted cross-laminated timber for the flooring in the stalls, boxes and the balcony, as well as a lower-carbon concrete for the transfer slabs. This is estimated to have saved approximately 220 tonnes of embodied carbon (CO2e) and captured a further 110 tonnes of CO2e for the life of the building by virtue of sequestered carbon, compared to a business-as-usual approach.
