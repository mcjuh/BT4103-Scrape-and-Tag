Early childhood development (ECD) is especially critical in fragile settings, yet funding and systemic support remain scarce. Evidence from Chad and Bangladesh shows refugee- and community-led models can deliver nurturing care, boost resilience, and have impact at scale: however, this requires sustained investment, integration into national systems, and a focus on safe home environments.

The developmental needs of young children do not pause during crises and shocks. Early deprivation can have long-lasting impacts on foundational learning, affecting both a [child’s lifelong learning potential](https://ijccep.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40723-023-00109-4) and [their wellbeing](https://www.unicef.org/media/158496/file/Global-report-on-early-childhoodcare-and-education-2024-1.pdf). Despite decades of advocacy and evidence, early childhood development (ECD) continues to receive limited attention in humanitarian settings. Global funding for education and early childhood development in emergencies remains critically low. In 2018, [only 2%](https://movingmindsalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/analysis-of-international-aid-levels-for-early-childhood-services-in-crisis-contexts-key-insights.pdf) of humanitarian aid supported early childhood development in emergencies (ECDiE) and this share hasn’t improved since. 
For children living through conflict or displacement, safe, nurturing, and stimulating early learning environments provide not only cognitive stimulation but psychosocial support. [Evidence](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ920516.pdf) demonstrates that investment in ECD yields some of the highest returns in human development, making it an effective strategy for long-term progress. [Investing in ECD can buffer toxic stress, improve attachment and emotional regulation, and lay the foundation for resilience](https://www.academia.edu/91888794/Understanding_and_Promoting_Resilience_in_the_Context_of_Adverse_Childhood_Experiences) among young children. As global aid evolves in response to shifting developmental, geopolitical, and climate-related challenges, prioritising ECD, particularly in conflict and displacement contexts, is both urgent and strategic. 
### Making it meaningful
Scaling nurturing environments for young children in humanitarian contexts, however, poses complex challenges. Such efforts often face unstable funding, fragmented delivery systems, and limited capacity within national systems, making it difficult to sustain and integrate ECD programmes. Additionally, many initiatives are short-term and siloed, failing to create a long-term, integrated support structure for young children.
Below we focus on insights from two unique programmes, drawing out lessons for the successful [scaling of ECD initiatives in conflict-affected and displacement settings](https://www.opml.co.uk/sites/default/files/2025-03/capability-statement-Education-in-Emergencies-web.pdf). The first is [Little Ripples](https://inee.org/resources/little-ripples-programme-chad) in eastern Chad: a low-cost, high-impact programme where refugee women and men are trained to adapt a [play-based, trauma-informed curriculum](https://www.opml.co.uk/projects/evaluating-innovations-to-scale-up-education-interventions-in-refugee-contexts). This refugee-led initiative provides education and psychosocial support while also creating livelihood opportunities. The second is a ‘Play-to-Learn’ programme in Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh. The programme, conducted as a ‘What Works’ study as part of our [Thrive programme](https://www.opml.co.uk/projects/thrive-early-childhood-development), uses a blended delivery model of weekly interactive voice response (IVR) messages and monthly home visits by trained facilitators to support parents in both Rohingya refugee camps and host communities. 
### 1. Meaningful localisation through refugee-led initiatives is both achievable and effective
Refugee-led and community-based models, when effectively supported, provide a highly relevant and sustainable approach to delivering ECD in crisis settings. The Little Ripples programme illustrates this well: refugee communities have demonstrated the insight, motivation, and social cohesion required to lead service delivery even in highly challenging environments. By identifying, recruiting, and training motivated refugee teachers and staff, the programme has delivered services aligned with global ECE standards while remaining responsive to local realities.
Too often, refugee communities are treated merely as beneficiaries of aid rather than active partners in its design and delivery. Evidence from Little Ripples shows that when trust and responsibility are transferred to local actors, programmes become more relevant, cost-effective, and resilient. By building on local assets and creating professional opportunities within the community, refugee-led models can provide high-quality ECD services that are sensitive to both cultural context and crisis conditions. At the same time, such models often face barriers to institutional recognition and struggle to access stable funding, particularly without formal organisational status. It is possible to address these challenges through strategic partnerships and long-term investment in training and mentoring for community staff, underscoring that refugee-led does not mean unsupported.
The Thrive study of the Play-to-Learn programme (in Cox’s Bazar) showed how locally recruited Learning Facilitators (LFs) could successfully build trust with Rohingya and host families and extend reach through blended delivery of IVR messages and home visits. This approach increased access, but findings also revealed gaps; facilitators often prioritised health messages over interactive play, and caregivers rarely practised activities with their children, limiting child engagement. The study highlights that while community-led delivery is both feasible and effective, it requires sustained training, closer supervision, and adapted tools to ensure fidelity and maximise impact. With the right investment and support, community-based models can unlock the full potential of ECD interventions, ensuring that even the most vulnerable children have the best start in life.
### 2. 'Refugee-led' does not mean 'unsupported' 
For refugee-led ECD initiatives to scale meaningfully, they need more than just frontline support. They require structured systems for management, accountability, professional development, sustained investments, and integration into wider systems, such as alignment with national health systems, social protection systems, teacher training, humanitarian coordination structures, etc. This can ensure that services are recognised, resourced, and connected rather than operating in isolation. The Little Ripples evaluation highlighted opportunities to strengthen programme governance, such as establishing a structured complaints mechanism, developing a documented risk management plan, and formalising roles for senior staff. Similarly, the ‘What Works’ study highlighted the importance of home visits and face-to-face communication, along with group sessions in refugee settings, for impactful engagement. In Cox’s Bazar, frontline LFs drawn from local communities were able to effectively communicate with both Rohingya and host families, including male household members who are often harder to reach.
Strengthening internal systems and linking them to broader service delivery ecosystems will be critical to ensuring both the quality and resilience of the programme. Donors and technical partners should explore how refugee-led and community-based models can strengthen the ECD humanitarian development nexus by recognising and integrating community members in the design and implementation, including them in national training programmes, and aligning initiatives with national ECD goals and curriculum standards.
### 3. Community-based innovations are opportunities for broader systems learning 
The Little Ripples Programme model demonstrated that scaling is not simply about replication. It also requires enabling conditions such as coordination with national policies, institutional partnerships that support legal and administrative operations, and sufficient ability within refugee communities to support quality at scale.
Complementing this, the ‘What Works’ study highlighted the potential of remote delivery methods in refugee settings to enhance child development in hard-to-reach populations. For example, caregivers who received guidance through IVR messages learned how to stimulate cognitive development by talking with their children, calling them by name, and spending quality time with them. 
In challenging contexts, fragile systems and limited data infrastructure further constrain the ability to track outcomes and advocate for scale. To address these challenges, future investments must support robust monitoring and evaluation (M&E) frameworks that generate child-level outcome data, document implementation fidelity, and create feedback loops between communities and implementers. These mechanisms are essential not only for adaptation but also for demonstrating impact and securing buy-in from national and international stakeholders. Consequently, scaling efforts should adopt a sequenced approach that strengthens systems and governance, establishes effective monitoring systems, and implements targeted advocacy with host governments to legitimise and embed refugee-led provision within national planning.
### 4. Sustainability depends on more than affordability and local delivery
The growing scale of the Little Ripples programme made its long-term sustainability more uncertain. Community demand quickly outpaced available financial and institutional capacity, while legal constraints on refugee status continued to limit programme autonomy. The partnership with the Jesuit Refugee Service (JRS), although enabling in some respects, also introduced ambiguity regarding governance and accountability. The Play-to-Learn programme in Cox’s Bazar also showed that sustainability depends on more than affordability and local delivery. While IVR messages offered a low-cost way to reach large numbers of families, their effectiveness relied heavily on consistent access to mobile phones, message comprehension, and follow-up through home visits. Without continued investment in facilitator training, supportive supervision, and adaptation of content, the benefits risk fading once donor funding ends. Moreover, high staff turnover and the absence of formal recognition for LFs limited the programme’s institutional stability. The blend of IVR messages and home visits showed promise in reaching large numbers of families, but also demonstrated that careful attention to deliver quality, message design, and cultural dynamics is essential for impact at scale.These findings highlight the importance of establishing long-term delivery systems, including integration into national training structures, developing pathways for community facilitators to obtain certification, and integrating content into existing health and education platforms. Together, these dynamics highlighted a broader challenge in refugee contexts: how to support community ownership while simultaneously building institutional structures that could ensure independence, scalability, and meaningful systems engagement.
### 5. Home environments matter
Evidence from the Play-to-Learn programme in Cox’s Bazar illustrated that parenting interventions can influence family dynamics more broadly. While most fathers reported increased positive interactions with children, surveys from the same study revealed that two-thirds engaged in psychological aggression and one-third in physical punishment. Nearly half of mothers reported experiencing intimate partner violence, with rates higher in Rohingya camps than in host communities. Encouragingly, exposure to the intervention improved mothers’ attitudes towards reducing violence against children, although reductions in actual violence were not yet observed. The evidence from this study further highlighted that child outcomes cannot be separated from the broader family environment. These findings emphasised that meaningful ECD programming in humanitarian settings must look beyond early learning and nutrition to address the conditions in which young children grow up. Integrating violence prevention, supporting maternal mental health, and strategies to engage fathers more systematically are not optional add-ons, but essential components of nurturing care. Programmes that create safe, supportive home environments are more likely to achieve lasting impacts on child development and resilience.
### Ways forward 
Without addressing structural barriers, locally grounded ECD programs in humanitarian settings will continue to remain short-term and their impact invisible in policy structures, unable to deliver the powerful and measurable outcomes for children’s development and well-being.
For donors, this is a call to action, not only to fund innovative models, but to reform the systems that exclude them. This requires designing financing mechanisms that enable service delivery models co-developed with targeted communities to access resources, embedding refugee perspectives in national policy dialogues, and creating pathways for innovations to evolve into stable and scalable components of national and humanitarian ECD systems. 
**This means rethinking how risk, accountability, and scale are defined in displacement contexts, as well as recognising the potential of refugee-led and community-based ECD service delivery models to shape not only resilient children, but also more resilient systems.**
