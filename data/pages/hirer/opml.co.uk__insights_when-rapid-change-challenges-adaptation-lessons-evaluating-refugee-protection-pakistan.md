Pakistan has hosted one of the world's largest Afghan refugee populations for more than four decades. That long history might suggest a settled, well-functioning protection system, but the reality on the ground tells a more complex story.
We are currently conducting an [independent mid-term evaluation](https://www.opml.co.uk/projects/evaluation-protection-and-solutions-afghan-refugees-pakistan) of UNHCR Pakistan's Multi-Year Country Strategy on Protection and Solutions for Afghan Refugees, Asylum Seekers, Host Communities, and Stateless Persons, supported by the Government of the Netherlands. As World Refugee Day prompts reflection on the state of global displacement, the evaluation is generating timely evidence on one of the most pressing challenges in humanitarian programming: what happens when the pace of change in a displacement context exceeds the capacity of programmes to adapt?
#### A rapidly shifting landscape
Pakistan is not a signatory to the 1951 Refugee Convention or its 1967 Protocol, and protection for Afghan nationals operates through a broader and evolving legal-administrative framework rather than dedicated refugee legislation. In recent months, that framework has shifted significantly. A policy of returning Afghan nationals to Afghanistan is now in effect – one that leaves limited room for negotiation and is moving faster than programming can keep pace with.
This is the central tension the evaluation is examining: not whether UNHCR's programme is adapting, but whether it can adapt quickly enough. The evaluation applies a theory-based mixed-methods design, drawing on desk review, quantitative beneficiary feedback, key informant interviews, focus group discussions, and operational insights from protection delivery and partner coordination structures. This combination of evidence allows the evaluation to assess not just what is being delivered, but how programme design assumptions are holding up against rapidly changing realities.
#### The human cost of quality at speed
Behind the policy shifts are people, and not a uniform population. One of the clearest observations emerging from the evaluation is that Afghan nationals are highly heterogeneous in their circumstances, vulnerabilities, and risks. This matters enormously for how protection programmes are designed and targeted.
Particular concern attaches to those facing multiple and intersecting vulnerabilities: people in mixed Afghan-Pakistani marriages, activists, transgender persons, and women in education all face heightened risks of forced repatriation and require specific consideration in programme design. These are not edge cases; they represent populations whose legal ambiguity and social visibility make them acutely exposed when policy environments harden.
The evaluation underscores that protection effectiveness is inseparable from the legal and administrative frameworks in which programmes operate. Without legal protection status, the reach of humanitarian actors is constrained in fundamental ways, no matter how well-resourced or well-intentioned the programme.
#### What stronger protection responses look like
Several cross-cutting priorities emerge from the evaluation's evidence that are relevant beyond Pakistan's specific context.
**Sustained and flexible funding is essential.** Uninterrupted support from international donors is urgently needed to maintain livelihoods, legal status, and protection from harassment, particularly when political attention to displacement shifts. Protracted displacement contexts require funding mechanisms that can sustain core protection services while remaining responsive to emerging risks.
**Coordination and legal advocacy must go hand in hand.** Increased coordination between UNHCR, its implementing partners, and government counterparts is critical, both for operational efficiency, and for supporting advocacy and identifying legal pathways for vulnerable and non-returnable Afghan nationals to remain in the country. Without legal protection, programmatic reach has a ceiling.
**Self-reliance programming must account for the full protection picture.** Livelihoods interventions cannot be designed in isolation from legal status, documentation constraints, and mobility restrictions. Portable livelihood skills, which remain useful sources of income generation regardless of where people move, offer a more resilient form of support in volatile policy environments.
**Voluntary repatriation support must be timely and rights-respecting.** Where repatriation is occurring, it is essential that time-sensitive assistance is in place to ensure it is safe, dignified, and genuinely voluntary. 
#### Evidence in service of adaptation
Evaluation has a particular role to play in contexts like Pakistan's as a tool for generating the independent evidence that adaptive programming depends on. When contexts change rapidly, evaluations help test whether assumptions still hold, surface emerging challenges before they become entrenched, and equip programme teams and donors with the evidence they need to make informed decisions.
The international community's sustained attention to displaced populations, regardless of shifting political priorities, is not just a moral imperative. It is a precondition for the kind of adaptive, evidence-informed programming that protects the most vulnerable. As displacement becomes increasingly protracted and complex, that commitment to both evidence and solidarity has never been more important.
