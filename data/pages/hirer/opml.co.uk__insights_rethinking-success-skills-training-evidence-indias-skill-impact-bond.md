India’s economic landscape is marked by both persistent challenges and significant opportunities. [Approximately 367 million Indians are aged 15–29 – one of the youngest workforces globally](https://azimpremjiuniversity.edu.in/publications/2026/report/swi-2026) – representing immense demographic potential. [However, only around 5% of this age group has received formal technical or vocational training](https://www.mospi.gov.in/uploads/publications_reports/publications_reports1780040415321_0624fb13-fb47-40bc-b470-7c7e9635c3ef_PLFS_2025_F_REV_29052026.pdf). This underscores a critical skills gap, which remains a key barrier to securing productive and sustained employment.
The challenge is not simply one of scale, however. Even where young people can access skills training, this does not consistently translate into meaningful employment. Placement rates across large-scale skills programmes often fall below 20%. For women, the picture is starker still, with placement rates typically falling below 15% and retention rates being even lower. With a female labour force participation rate of 31%, persistent barriers remain around mobility, safety, and family consent, which prevent many women from entering paid work at all. Of those who do complete skills training programmes, only around one in 10 remain in employment three months later. These challenges are exacerbated by the fact that there is limited evidence on the kinds of career trajectories that are possible after training, and on the factors that prevent young people from obtaining jobs and remaining employed.
The [Skill Impact Bond](https://nsdcindia.org/sib) was designed to address precisely these challenges. Central to its design is a strong emphasis on monitoring, evaluation, and learning: not just to track results, but to generate in-depth evidence on how skills training programmes function in practice, where the fault lines lie, and how that knowledge can be used to support the ecosystem to deliver better outcomes.
#### What has the programme achieved so far?
Launched in 2021, the Skill Impact Bond is the first of its kind and the largest development impact bond globally in the skills and employment sector. It targets 50,000 young people (62% of whom must be young women) over a four-year period, across high-growth sectors including retail, apparel, capital goods, and IT-enabled services. It is implemented across eight cohorts of trainees, allowing for continuous improvement and adaptation over time. 
What sets it apart from conventional skills training programmes in India is how success has been redefined through a focus on outcomes rather than inputs. At the core of the programme lies an outcomes-based financing model. Rather than measuring inputs and outputs only – such as number of enrolments, training days, and certifications issued – the programme ties funding to three outcomes that reflect whether skills training has actually worked: Were trainees successfully certified? Did they subsequently join a job? And did they remain in that job three months later? 
To make this model work, the programme brings together a coalition of stakeholders with aligned incentives. Risk investors provide upfront working capital to training partners, with repayment by outcome funders linked to independently verified outcomes. Payments are structured on a sliding scale, with higher returns tied to stronger performance in job placement and retention. This mechanism aligns incentives across partners, requiring them to prioritise employment outcomes, rather than just delivering skills training. 
As the [monitoring, evaluation, and learning partner for this programme](https://www.opml.co.uk/projects/assessing-the-impact-of-skill-impact-bond-in-india), our role has been to verify three payment-linked outcomes: certification, job placement, and three months’ job retention. However, beyond verification, for the first time in a programme of this scale, we have collected in-depth data on the post-training trajectories of young people, supporting partners to drive concurrent programme improvements and strengthening accountability.
#### What does the evidence tell us so far?
Halfway through the programme, the results are meaningful, particularly for women.
Young women make up over 70% of trainees enrolled in Cohorts I to IV, surpassing the programme’s 60% target and demonstrating that targeted outreach can successfully engage young women, who are often excluded from skills training and labour market participation. 
Beyond access, skills and employment outcomes for women have improved significantly over time. Across the first four cohorts, certification rates are consistently high for women, reaching 92% in Cohort IV. Job placement has improved steadily to 81%. On-the-job retention, which is arguably the most difficult yet crucial outcome to achieve, shows the most notable improvement, increasing from 48% in Cohort I to 66% in Cohort IV. This suggests that the programme is increasingly supporting young women in translating their skills training into sustained employment. 
We have also seen early evidence of the programme supporting women’s self-employment: the share of female trainees in self-employment has risen from 6% in Cohort I to 14% in Cohort IV, particularly among those trained in entrepreneurial trades such as tailoring and small retail. 
Men’s outcomes have remained strong throughout, with certification rates of over 90% across all cohorts, placement rates between 80% and 84%, and job retention improving from 56% in Cohort II to 66% in Cohort IV. The gap between men’s and women’s job placement outcomes, which was 17 percentage points in Cohort I, has narrowed to just 3% for Cohort IV. 
#### What challenges persist?
Strong programme outcomes do not tell the whole story, however, and certain challenges remain.
Occupational segregation remains pronounced. Women are still concentrated in selected sectors – primarily apparel manufacturing – though this is slowly changing, with growing numbers entering IT-enabled services. While some female trainees do enrol in courses corresponding to sectors such as banking, financial services and insurance, retail, telecom, and even electronics (a traditionally male-dominated field), their participation remains limited. Men continue to dominate work in the construction sector, with some gradual movement into the automotive and capital goods sectors. This indicates that, while progress is being made, sectoral pathways remain gendered, which can influence both earning potential and career mobility. 
The income gap between men and women also persists. Male trainees consistently report higher monthly salaries than female trainees – with a difference of approximately INR 900 to INR 2,700 per month – and there is no clear sign of this narrowing across cohorts. This difference is partly linked to the concentration of women in traditionally feminised sectors such as the apparel sector, which tend to offer lower median monthly salaries. However, our site visits revealed something more granular: even when employed within the same sector, men and women had differentiated roles, with more lucrative roles (e.g. sales roles with a high incentive component) typically occupied by men. Women also reported facing greater barriers to career progression, due either to discrimination or to the need to take temporary exits from the workforce because of their gender-based responsibilities (e.g. for maternity leave or caregiving). Finally, due to their domestic roles (such as tending to an ill family member, etc.), women tended to take more leave than their male colleagues. Combined, these factors contributed to women earning a lower monthly salary. 
What is more, despite the programme’s success in supporting women into employment, socio-cultural and structural constraints continue to hinder women’s participation in paid work after skills training. Across the first four cohorts, 22–33% of women reported household responsibilities or lack of family consent as barriers to taking up jobs after completing skills training. Others cited education-related reasons (13–20%) or unsuitable job locations (10–17%), pointing to the need for more flexible, local, and aspirational employment pathways for young women. 
#### Looking ahead
The evidence so far points to a clear conclusion: outcomes-based financing, when combined with rigorous monitoring, evaluation, and learning systems, adaptive programme design, and strong partnerships, helps improve employability outcomes for young people, particularly women. 
Perhaps equally significant, however, is the body of evidence generated by the programme. For the first time, we have in-depth data on how young people navigate a combination of skills gaps, aspiration gaps, and information gaps to realise their goals. Using rigorous mixed-methods research (including longitudinal studies focusing on in-depth post-placement tracking of candidates), we now have data on what works and where the challenges lie ([see the results from the mid-term verification findings from the Skill Impact Bond](https://nsdcindia.org/sites/default/files/SkillImpactBondResults_MidTermReport_17072025.pdf)). This body of evidence has enormous potential and could be used by policymakers, training providers, and funders to design innovative programmes, strengthen the monitoring and evaluation systems of existing skills training programmes, and drive real-time improvements. 
The fact that the Skill Impact Bond has a group of passionate, committed partners is one of the programme’s greatest strengths. Our experience with traditional evaluations is that engagement with evidence ends once reports are submitted and payments have been made. What has been particularly rewarding for us here is that, with the Skill Impact Bond, stakeholders continue to actively discuss our reports months after they have been generated, coming up with new research questions, seeking answers, requesting customised learning workshops, and repurposing the material we have created to begin new conversations on the future of skills training programmes in India. 
A key insight from this work is that impact depends on more than just measurement. It requires moving beyond a transactional focus on programme performance towards a genuine, shared inquiry into how evidence can drive change. We don’t have all the answers, but we do have something valuable: a detailed picture of what young people in India actually experience as they move from home to training centre to workplace, often thousands of kilometres away, in the space of just three or four months. We have tracked the journey of candidates as they transition from the training centre to the workplace, and the evidence highlights the kinds of career pathways short-term skills training can provide, as well as pointing to where these pathways could end. 
We are fortunate to be at this inspiring stage in our journey as the monitoring, evaluation, and learning partner on the Skill Impact Bond. Over the next few months, we will be working with our partners to go beyond sharing lessons learned, and towards using this evidence to design and implement new scaled-up programmes that maximise the impact of skills and livelihoods interventions in India.
