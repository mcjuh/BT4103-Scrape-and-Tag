The risk management function helps protect assets and enables the continuity of business operations. This can include operating a captive insurance company as a funding mechanism for self-insuring risk.
Adaptable and diligent oversight of captive programs can be vital to parent companies realizing their operational and financial goals. However, many captives often lack a governance framework that properly identifies and formalizes processes and controls across all of the captive’s coverages, thereby compromising operational transparency, internal controls effectiveness, efficiency and performance.

  2. Roles and responsibilities
  3. Vendor management
  4. Financial and operational controls


### Organizational structure
**Organizational structure** forms a captive’s foundation and is critical to establishing important governance areas (e.g., roles and responsibilities). Individuals involved in other functional roles and reporting chains within the parent operate and manage most captives, but you should treat your captive organization as a standalone company that requires an independent organizational chart.
When creating or updating your organizational chart, be sure to include individuals’ titles, clearly indicating titles within both the captive and the parent. While this may seem contrary to the above, providing parent company titles in addition to captive titles can help you determine congruence in financial controls and the approvals necessary to operate both the parent and captive. In addition, it’s common to include key vendors such as the captive manager and claims administrator(s)’ contacts within the organizational chart to help gain a broader picture of the operation.
### Roles and responsibilities
**Roles and responsibilities** often lack clear definition and timely updates. Captive bylaws typically outline executive officer roles and responsibilities, but they’re most often high-level and unspecific. A roles and responsibilities document should contain detailed job descriptions—similar to job postings—of all active captive team members, including the executive team and vendors. Clear roles and responsibilities greatly facilitate documenting processes, define controls and help to create an effective business succession plan. If your captive lacks this documentation, consider having all individuals create their own job descriptions, remembering to include only the captive-related activities (exclusive of any existing parent company responsibilities). Also have them provide or update their career biographies in a resume or curriculum vitae format to help leadership identify any significant gaps between stated roles and responsibilities and current experience and capabilities.
A review of all captive roles and respective responsibilities may uncover an inappropriate mingling of duties, the need for stronger financial controls and a lack of consistent oversight of insurance programs. The review also can support any decisions to expand or reduce headcount, as well as determine succession planning. There are many captives that have existed for decades with minimal scrutiny of staffing levels and overhead. In these circumstances, a change in leadership typically requires extensive and time-consuming operational review by new management. By having clear and up-to-date role descriptions, it’s much easier to recognize the competencies, configuration and skill set of the team.
### Vendor management
**Vendor management** is an area that captive stakeholders often overlook or downplay. We hear from many captives that they’ve been doing business with particular vendors for many years and have no reason to consider changing them. It’s good to have strong, lasting relationships, but most likely none of your vendors are irreplaceable. You should at least be aware of other options that could actually fit the needs of your business. If the parent company has procurement protocols in place, consider adopting them. If its protocols aren’t applicable or too restrictive, then create your own.
Moving to a new vendor can be relatively easy if you take the time to think about what you expect out of each vendor in both quantitative and qualitative terms. We advise clients to consider a request for proposal (RFP) for key vendors every three to five years. That said, you should base RFP frequency on your ability to evaluate performance. While most service level agreements may have some embedded performance standards (e.g., turnaround times), most do not include genuine performance metrics. Be sure to ask in RFPs how a new vendor will plan and carry out migration of services and data and how you can hold vendors accountable for meeting their schedule and goals. Lastly, vendor contract renewal and bid decisions shouldn’t be the purview of just one individual within the captive organization. Make sure there’s broad involvement so all relevant stakeholders are aware of potential change and their voices are heard.
### Financial and operational controls
**Financial and operational controls** are typically the areas in which parent companies and captives require the most guidance. PwC receives calls from internal governance, risk and control teams who are concerned that, as a captive has grown and changed, existing controls may be inadequate.
Shortcomings in captive operations may fly under the radar on a one-off basis but can accumulate over time and become real headaches. For example, premiums sent from the parent to the captive’s fronting carrier or broker may not be remitted to the captive. The fronting carrier or broker may inadvertently remit net premium to the parent rather than to the captive, which can result in the funds sitting in unapplied cash with the parent. This lack of financial controls (namely, the recording of premiums receivable and timely follow up when payment has not been received) can lead to financial reporting misstatements.
There also can be operational controls deficiencies in claims oversight, a critical part of the captive’s responsibility. For example, without operational controls, such as maintaining secondary approvers on all communications or having a general captive e-mail address that’s carbon copied on all claim correspondence, processes can break down, resulting in real financial consequences—including plaintiff lawsuits, costly litigation and ultimately larger claims payouts than necessary. 
## How PwC can help
PwC’s risk modeling services practice assists captive and self-insured clients with traditional pricing and reserving, data management and analytics, claims management leading practices and corporate governance, also offering innovative modernization solutions solutions.
We help clients assess and improve their captive governance by identifying and formalizing the process and controls across coverages and major insurance functions (including claims, reserving, pricing, funding and underwriting). We prepare detailed documentation on the processes in place for each coverage. We help clients identify controls owners and determine how they can effectively attest to completeness and effectiveness on a regular, scheduled basis. If errors surface, we can trace and isolate relevant facts and circumstances and suggest tangible improvements. 
Our approach is to first identify each key process. Through detailed working sessions with key stakeholders, we can capture and document the workflow process. This helps reveal where and with whom risks and controls do or should reside. We then can create a logical risk control matrix for each key process. Finally, we marry each process diagram with its respective matrix into a narrative that tells the story of process, people and controls. This can help you improve your captive’s effectiveness, demonstrate good governance, facilitate the onboarding of new staff and satisfy internal audit requirements.
