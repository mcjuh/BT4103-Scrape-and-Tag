#  There’s an executive role that makes AI actually work. Here are three signs your enterprise doesn’t have one. 
Enterprises have invested heavily in AI, yet most still struggle to turn capability into business performance. The missing link may not be another tool, but a different way of leading the system.
### The executive brief
  * Only 13% of enterprises have converted AI investment into operational impact.
  * Most organizations are still stuck in “random acts of AI.”
  * [A new paper from Futurum](https://futurumgroup.com/ibm-orchestrator-hub/) explores why enterprise AI needs an orchestrator to align the business around shared outcomes.


##  Introduction 
Enterprises are [spending more in AI](https://newsroom.ibm.com/blog-why-the-ai-boom-is-running-into-a-cost-reckoning) than ever, yet results remain stubbornly elusive. According to new research from Futurum¹, only 13% have reached the tipping point where it actually changes how they operate. AI has spread across the enterprise faster than leadership structures can adapt, leaving critical decisions and accountability fragmented. The gap between what the business is building and what it’s actually delivering continues to widen, and more technology won’t close it. In my role as Data and Hybrid Cloud Leader for IBM Consulting Americas, I often see what I call “random acts of AI”. These are initiatives that work brilliantly inside a single function but never connect to outcomes that meaningfully move the business. Each system is doing its job. The results still disappoint. It doesn’t have to be that way.

At IBM, we applied AI, automation and workflow redesign across our own global operations to test whether orchestration at enterprise scale actually delivers. It did: [USD 4.5 billion in annualized productivity savings](https://www.ibm.com/case-studies/ibm-client-zero), HR operating costs reduced by 40%, and IT support volumes cut by 74%. The technology was already there. What unlocked value was treating the enterprise as a system, with clear ownership over how all the pieces connect. In our client engagements, the organizations pulling ahead have stopped asking how to deploy more AI and started asking how to orchestrate what they already have. A new paper from Futurum suggests the challenge for most leadership teams is determining whether that coordination truly exists across their enterprise—and who should own it.
##  Three signs to look for 
1. You’ve invested in platforms. The outcomes aren’t following. 
Many CXOs have already placed major platform bets—Salesforce, SAP, Adobe, Oracle, AWS—and those decisions aren’t in question. What has changed is the expectation. These systems are now expected to contribute to business outcomes that extend well beyond their original design. Pricing, inventory and customer commitments each live in different systems. When those systems don’t move in sync, enterprise operations pull in opposing directions. Teams, priorities and resources become harder to coordinate, making it more difficult to execute consistently and deliver results at scale. The outcomes the business cares about don’t live in any single platform. They depend on how platforms coordinate. In orchestrated enterprises, platforms are aligned around shared outcomes rather than operating as independent systems.
2. Systems are connected but not coordinated. 
This is the distinction many enterprises miss. Integration connects systems so they can exchange information. Orchestration determines whether those systems produce outcomes together. Many enterprises have made meaningful progress on the first. Far fewer have achieved the second. What often emerges instead are those “random acts of AI” I mentioned earlier: initiatives optimized for their own corner of the business but disconnected from a shared outcome. The systems may be performing exactly as designed. But nobody is conducting them together, so value remains unrealized. In leading enterprises, coordination is explicit. Rather than relying on large, disruptive transformation programs, enterprises evolve continuously as initiatives reinforce one another and turn isolated wins into repeatable performance.
3. No one can answer, “Who’s conducting? 
As AI spreads across the enterprise, responsibility for activating and governing it is distributed across the executive team—technology, finance, operations, HR, security—and every role owns a piece. In theory, that coverage feels comprehensive. In practice, it creates a leadership structure where everyone assumes someone else has the full picture. The CTO owns infrastructure and capability, the CFO owns return and risk, and the COO owns execution and efficiency. Each is doing exactly what their role demands. Yet when AI initiatives stall or ROI is questioned in a board meeting, it becomes difficult to pinpoint who’s accountable—not because anyone failed, but because accountability was never clearly assigned in the first place. Nearly half of enterprises cite unclear business value or ROI as a top barrier to generative AI adoption. Ambiguity at the leadership level compounds quickly. When enterprises discover they don’t quite know who to ask, what emerges is a mosaic of responses that don’t fully cohere. The organizations delivering real AI outcomes have a clear answer: the Orchestrator—a leader or function with the visibility, authority and capability to align the entire system and guide it toward a shared outcome. The role already exists in some organizations, just without the title. In many others, it’s the gap nobody has named yet.
##  The coordination advantage 
When the enterprise is treated as a system—with clear ownership over how its parts operate as a system—data, AI, platforms and workflows begin reinforcing each other instead of competing for attention. The playbook IBM applied internally is the same one we help enterprises build—not as a technology implementer, but as the coordinating function that maintains a whole-system view when internal structures make that difficult. This is the challenge many enterprises now face. The question has shifted from what to invest in to how to make those investments work together to produce outcomes. The window to get this right is narrower than many leadership teams realize. I believe the decisions made over the next 12 to 24 months will shape both near-term performance and long-term competitiveness. The enterprises getting this right aren’t necessarily the ones spending the most on technology. They’re the ones that have deliberately built the coordination capability that makes technology work. That decision is available to every enterprise. The question is who makes it first.
