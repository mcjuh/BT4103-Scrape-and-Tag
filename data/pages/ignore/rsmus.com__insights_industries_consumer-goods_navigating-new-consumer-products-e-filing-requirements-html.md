# Navigating new consumer products e-filing requirements
### What importers must do
July 01, 2026
##  Key takeaways 
Consumer product importers must shift to real-time e-filing at entry.
Companies must submit structured certificate data via ACE for consumer product shipments.
Success requires aligned data, systems and supply chain coordination.
Consumer goods
The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) is fundamentally changing how import compliance is demonstrated for consumer products entering the United States.
[Beginning July 8, 2026](https://www.cpsc.gov/eFiling), importers will be required to electronically submit product certificate data at the time of customs entry—marking a shift from a document-on-demand model to proactive, data-driven submission, enabling CPSC to conduct real-time risk assessments and admissibility determinations.
For consumer products organizations with global supply chains, this move introduces new operational, data and compliance considerations that extend beyond a simple filing change.
## What is changing—and why it matters
Compliance is shifting from a reactive, document-based process to a proactive, data-driven requirement integrated with customs entry**.** Historically, importers have been required to maintain Certificates of Compliance and provide them only upon request. Under the new framework, that approach is no longer sufficient.
Instead, importers must electronically transmit certificate data through the U.S. Customs and Border Protection’s Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) system at the time of entry.
This change is part of a broader CPSC modernization effort designed to enhance product safety oversight by enabling pre-arrival risk screening, improving the targeting of high-risk shipments and facilitating faster release of compliant goods.
## Who is affected
The requirement applies broadlyto importers of all qualifying imports (i.e., finished consumer products subject to CPSC safety rules, standards, bans or regulations), including de minimis (low value) shipments. This includes children’s products requiring a Children’s Product Certificate (CPC) and general-use consumer products requiring a General Certificate of Conformity (GCC), covering a wide range of commonly traded goods such as toys, furniture, household items, apparel and certain electronics, spanning a wide range of approximately 600 HTS codes (Harmonized Tariff Schedule). It does not alter which products require certification, only how and when the data must be submitted.
While customs brokers may transmit filings, the importer remains legally responsible for data accuracy and completeness.
## Key data requirements and filing pathways
Under e-filing requirements, importers must submit structured data extracted from their certificates of compliance. Required elements generally include:
  * Product identification (e.g., SKU, model number, UPC)
  * Applicable CPSC safety regulations and standards
  * Certifier (importer) information
  * Recordkeeper contact details
  * Manufacturing date and location
  * Testing laboratory information and testing dates
  * Certification statement (attestation of compliance) 


These requirements reinforce the need for traceability across the supply chain, testing and certification records, particularly in multicountry manufacturing environments.
CPSC provides two primary methods for submitting required data, including:
  * Full Partner Government Agency (PGA) Message set: All certificate data is transmitted with each entry filing via ACE. The customs broker typically handles this upon receipt of the importer’s documentation.
  * Product registry (reference filing): Importers upload certificate data once into the CPSC Product Registry and reuse a reference identifier for subsequent shipments. For companies with repeat SKUs or standardized product lines, the registry can significantly reduce the need for repetitive data submissions but requires upfront data alignment and governance.


## Operational and compliance implications for importers
The new requirements introduce several practical challenges:
  * Data readiness and quality: Many organizations store certificate data in decentralized formats (PDFs, spreadsheets, supplier files). E-filing requires this data to be standardized, complete and system-ready.
  * Supply chain coordination: Critical data points often reside with overseas suppliers or labs, requiring tighter coordination on testing protocols and certificate generation.
  * Systems integration: Organizations must align internal enterprise resource planning systems and compliance tools with customs brokers and the CPSC product registry to ensure timely data transmission at entry.
  * Compliance risk management: Incomplete or inaccurate filings may result in customs holds or delays, increased inspections, potential penalties or refused entry.


**Preparing for implementation**
With the July 2026 deadline in effect, importers should prioritize:
  * Validating product scope: Identify which SKUs require CPC or GCC certification.
  * Centralizing certificate data: Ensure all required data elements are complete and accessible.
  * Defining filing processes: Align with customs brokers on transmission method (full vs. registry).


Companies that treat e-filing as a data and process transformation initiative—not just a compliance task—will be better positioned to avoid disruption.
