# Finance execution unlocks AI value at scale
Finance execution maturity—not technology—unlocks scalable AI value, transforming core finance processes through disciplined operating models.
[Download the insights (will open in a new tab)](https://www.ibm.com/downloads/documents/us-en/16951f13b4e17217)[Subscribe for more insights from IBM IBV (will open in a new window)](https://www.ibm.com/thought-leadership/institute-business-value/en-us/newsletters)
Artificial intelligence is no longer an emerging capability in finance. It is already reshaping how work gets done—cutting costs, accelerating cycles, and improving accuracy across core finance processes. Yet as AI adoption grows, performance gaps between organizations are widening rather than narrowing.
The difference is not access to better models or more advanced tools. It is execution.
New research from the IBM Institute for Business Value (IBV), conducted in partnership with the American Productivity & Quality Center, shows that finance organizations achieving the strongest and most durable AI returns are not those deploying the most use cases. They are those embedding AI deeply into execution—redesigning workflows, standardizing data, and governing AI as an operational capability rather than a set of pilots.
Among experienced adopters, AI is already delivering a median 8% reduction in total annual finance cost, with reductions reaching 18% where AI is built directly into end‑to‑end processes rather than siloed use cases. As finance leaders push AI from experimentation into daily operations, execution discipline—not technology choice—has become the decisive factor. 
> Finance organizations achieving the strongest and most durable AI returns are not those deploying the most use cases. They are those embedding AI deeply into execution.
###  From experimentation to execution: why sequencing matters
AI adoption in finance is uneven by design—and that unevenness is a signal, not a flaw.
IBV research shows that high‑performing finance organizations sequence AI deployment, scaling first in domains where data is already standardized and workflows are repetitive, while deliberately investing to prepare other areas for future scale. This sequencing approach consistently outperforms broad, simultaneous rollout strategies.
The pattern is clear across the four core finance domains: 
  * **Financial planning and analysis (FP &A)** leads early adoption, reflecting structured data, recurring planning cycles, and well‑defined metrics
  * **Order‑to‑cash (O2C) and record‑to‑report (R2R)** follow, where transaction flows and control frameworks support embedded AI
  * **Procure‑to‑pay (P2P)** lags, constrained by fragmented supplier data and upstream complexity


Among organizations that have reached the highest levels of AI maturity, adoption reaches 95% in FP&A, but only 58% in P2P. This gap does not reflect lower ambition—it reflects disciplined prioritization. Leaders scale AI where it can deliver measurable value today, while building the data, integration, and governance foundations required elsewhere.
The same logic applies to advanced AI. Generative AI adoption concentrates in analytics‑ and narrative‑heavy work such as forecasting and reporting, where outputs can be governed and tied to clear deliverables. Early agentic AI adoption appears in bounded, transaction‑heavy workflows—particularly in O2C—where the autonomy of AI agents can be constrained by rules, monitoring, and escalation paths. 
### Where AI in finance pays off—and why execution maturity wins
AI is already delivering structural efficiency gains across the finance function. But the magnitude, speed, and reliability of those gains depend on execution maturity.
Across the sample, experienced adopters report a median 8% reduction in total finance cost, while organizations with more mature execution models report reductions approaching 18%. These gains do not come from isolated automation. They come from embedding AI into redesigned workflows where decision-making, controls, and data flows are simplified first.
Execution maturity also reshapes delivery economics: 
  * Deployment cost falls by roughly 30% as maturity increases, dropping from about USD59 to USD39 per employee.
  * Payback time improves by around 25%, declining from roughly eight months to six.
  * Time to deploy shortens, reflecting repeatable delivery models rather than one‑off projects.


Process‑level outcomes reinforce the pattern. In FP&A, AI reduces manual effort while improving forecast reliability. In O2C, organizations report fewer uncollectables, faster invoice‑to‑cash cycles, and lower days sales outstanding. In R2R, cost per journal entry declines while close cycles shorten and first‑time accuracy improves. Even in P2P, where scale is harder, experienced adopters report gains in invoice processing cost and approval cycle time.
What differentiates these outcomes is not the use case itself, but whether AI is embedded into execution—supported by controls, monitoring, and clear exception management—rather than layered onto fragmented processes. 
### The operating model gap holding finance back
Despite proven returns, many organizations struggle to scale AI consistently. The obstacles are rarely technical.
Finance leaders cite a familiar set of barriers: concerns around AI reliability, compliance, and regulation; limited AI skills capacity; poor data quality and fragmented systems; and integration complexity across legacy platforms. These gaps explain why AI value compounds in some organizations and stalls in others.
The research highlights a critical insight: what matters most is not how many AI use cases are deployed, but how they are deployed. Many finance processes were designed for manual execution across disconnected systems, with periodic review and heavy handoffs. Applying AI on top of these workflows accelerates inefficiency rather than eliminating it.
High‑performing finance organizations take a different approach. They redesign workflows before embedding AI—clarifying decision points, standardizing inputs, reducing handoffs, and defining how AI outputs trigger action. Trust, auditability, and exception handling are designed into the workflow from the start, protecting both ROI and credibility as AI scales.
> High‑performing finance organizations redesign workflows before embedding AI—clarifying decision points, standardizing inputs, reducing handoffs, and defining how AI outputs trigger action.
**Action guide**
Building durable AI advantage in finance
As AI becomes embedded in core finance execution, CFOs and leaders must own architecture and operating‑model decisions. IBV research identifies three moves finance leaders can make now to turn AI into a repeatable source of value.**1. Design for return, not pilots** High‑return organizations treat AI as a portfolio of process outcomes—not a collection of tools. They build a multi‑year finance AI blueprint aligned to enterprise AI strategy and deliberately sequence deployment across financial planning and analysis (FP&A), order‑to‑cash (O2C), procure‑to‑pay (P2P), and record‑to‑report (R2R) based on execution readiness.
Anchor each use case to a primary value driver—cost, cycle time, or quality—supported by a small set of secondary metrics. This discipline enables teams to compare initiatives, prioritise investment, and scale or stop efforts based on evidence rather than enthusiasm. 
**2. Solidify the foundation for scale** High‑return organizations treat data, trust, and lifecycle management as prerequisites—not afterthoughts. They invest in governed, reusable finance data foundations, explicitly define data requirements for priority workflows, and embed explainability, auditability, and controls directly into delivery.
Institutionalize model lifecycle management through regular or real-time continuous review, preventing performance drift and sustaining value as data and requirements evolve. Fund integration and platform modernization—particularly in P2P—as part of the AI value case, rather than deferring it as background IT work. 
**3. Industrialize execution** Organizations that achieve faster payback and lower delivery costs move beyond project‑based delivery. They build an execution engine that repeatedly converts ideas into production outcomes.
Establish formal use‑case intake, assign accountable AI product owners by finance process, conduct regular ROI reviews, and define clear Finance–IT decision rights. Embed adoption mechanisms—training, updated operating procedures, and usage monitoring—into every production release, making AI part of how finance runs day to day.
### AI value in finance is no longer constrained by technology. It is constrained by execution.
Organizations that sequence AI intentionally, redesign workflows before automating them, and anchor AI in a finance‑led operating model turn early gains into sustained advantage—not just for finance, but for the entire enterprise.

**FAQ**
Why does execution maturity matter more than technology choice for AI in finance?
The research shows that AI outcomes in finance are driven by how deeply AI is embedded into workflows, not by tools alone. Organizations with mature execution models achieve faster payback—around six months versus eight—and significantly lower delivery costs, demonstrating that operating discipline, not technology selection, determines scalable AI value.
What financial impact does AI deliver when scaled across finance operations?
Experienced AI adopters report a median 8% reduction in total finance costs, rising to as much as 18% when AI is embedded end to end across finance processes rather than deployed in isolated use cases. This shows that scale—not pilots—unlocks the largest economic gains.
Where should finance leaders prioritize AI deployment first?
The research finds that AI adoption is highest where data is standardized and workflows are repeatable. Among optimizing adopters, AI penetration reaches 95% in financial planning and analysis, compared with just 58% in procure-to-pay, signalling where finance teams should scale AI now and where to build prerequisites first.
How does operating model maturity affect AI economics and speed to value?
Organizations with mature AI execution models not only achieve faster payback but also reduce delivery costs by around 30%, with median costs dropping from about USD59 to USD39 per employee. This highlights how industrialized execution improves both speed and economics as AI scales.
Why are governance and data foundations critical to scaling AI in finance?
The report shows AI scale often stalls due to gaps in trust, data quality, integration and governance. High‑performing finance teams design controls, auditability and data discipline directly into workflows, enabling AI to deliver repeatable, credible results across regulated, mission‑critical finance processes.
