# Short runway, big payoff
How can the retail revolution unlock double-digit gains for airlines?
[Download the insights (will open in a new tab)](https://www.ibm.com/downloads/documents/us-en/1443d5cdd6c02c7e)[Subscribe for more insights from IBM IBV (will open in a new window)](https://www.ibm.com/thought-leadership/institute-business-value/en-us/newsletters)
Airlines have long aspired to become great retailers. If they can deliver passenger experiences that mirror what customers enjoy in other sectors, improved revenue and profit margins can flow. Yet rigid legacy systems and uncertain paths to progress have frustrated these ambitions. Airline executives can’t help but wonder, what will it take to succeed this time around?
Already, cutting-edge airline distribution solutions are reshaping how the airline industry brings their products to market—enabling them to personalize offers, engage customers directly, and streamline travel experiences. Airlines can now rearchitect customer offers, orders, settlement, and delivery—OOSD for short—enabled by the New Distribution Capability (NDC) and ONE Order, which are modern data transmission standards developed by the International Air Transport Association (IATA).
While these evolving standards are by no means perfect, they offer a path for better, cheaper, more personalized air travel distribution, all while increasing strategic flexibility, improving decision-making, and eliminating vendor lock-in.
> Airlines can now bundle optimally priced offers targeted to individual customer interest, preferences, and loyalty.
With more service-oriented platforms and new technologies, airlines can also improve customer satisfaction, grow revenue, and reduce operational and IT complexity. Longstanding aviation industry data anchors, including passenger name records (PNRs), tickets, and electronic miscellaneous documents (EMDs), will become redundant and irrelevant.
In the offer and order management domains, the emerging solutions have more in common with e-commerce technologies than traditional airline systems and are better suited to interact with AI chatbots to drive revenue. And advancements in offer and order management solutions enable the move to retail-hardened solutions for settlement and delivery, helping airlines reduce both cost and complexity. They also help airlines deliver more consistently and settle with partners and other providers in more timely and accurate ways.
> More than 90% of airlines expect to make widespread changes to their distribution systems by 2030.
To better understand this critical moment—and how airlines are responding to evolving technology trends—the IBM Institute for Business Value surveyed 200 leaders from large global airlines in the second and third quarters of 2025, in partnership with Oxford Economics.
What business results can airlines expect from modernization? According to our research, airlines that say their order management transformation initiatives are almost complete expect up to 17% higher revenue improvements and up to 24% greater cost reductions from these efforts. Airline executives also anticipate improved customer intimacy and satisfaction (69%), increased revenue growth (66%), and reduced dependence on a single IT provider (48%).
The advantage early leaders have gained is likely to compound if other airlines continue to delay forward-looking upgrades or stand on the sidelines waiting to see how this industry transformation will unfold. 
**Percentages reflect financial improvements executives expect across OOSD solutions by 2030.**
### Time is of the essence.
More than 90% of airlines expect to make widespread changes to their distribution systems by 2030. And according to our research, some are far ahead: roughly 40% of airlines have already made big strides toward transforming their OOSD solutions.
By shortening time-to-market for new promotions, allocating resources more strategically, and optimizing cost structures as market conditions change, forward-looking airlines are building a clear competitive advantage. Integrating OOSD systems also lets airlines provide seamless customer experiences.
> Airline CxOs cite legacy systems as the top barrier limiting modernization and thwarting innovation.
What will it take for airlines to operate more like modern retailers? It doesn’t have to be al all-or-nothing gamble. By prioritizing specific areas of improvement and tackling business model changes incrementally, airlines can minimize disruption to their existing operations while still making meaningful progress.
For example, focusing on AI-powered personalization can help build the customer loyalty that delivers better returns. Or rolling out a mobile-first platform for delivering travel documents can improve the travel experience by letting customers check-in and access boarding passes, hotel vouchers, and rental car confirmations on their mobile devices.
Artificial intelligence also gives airlines the opportunity to combine analytics from customer relationship management (CRM) systems with data from their ERP platforms to inform each customer interaction, building offers that adequately account for both demand and overall customer value. This level of customization lets airlines bundle offers in a way that will be most attractive to individual travelers—at their ideal price point. In addition to boosting sales in slow periods, offer personalization can help improve Net Promoter Scores (NPS) and ancillary revenue per passenger.
> Improved customer intimacy and satisfaction and increased revenue growth are the top benefits airline executives expect from transformation.
Another top priority is integrating passenger data across touch points, including third-party retailing platforms, which lets airlines offer tailored ancillary products and dynamic pricing. Hyper-segmentation and personalized pricing have been elusive goals for most airlines, but AI-driven capabilities finally put them within reach. With emerging technologies and standards, advanced data analytics, and machine learning algorithms airlines can optimize pricing and revenue management, considering factors such as demand, competition, and customer behavior.
