Most smart TA programmes have two clients. Too often, the one that matters most gets served last. Three OPM practitioners outline why putting government first is not just good ethics, but is what makes technical assistance work.

Every technical assistance programme has two clients.
The first holds the contract. They set the terms of reference, approve the workplan, and sign off the deliverables. They measure success through log frame indicators, annual reviews, and milestone reports. They are, in the formal sense, the client.
The second client is the recipient – typically a government ministry or department — that receives the Technical Assistance (TA). They did not directly commission the work but ideally are involved in shaping it. They may go on to use what the programme produces or quietly set it aside. Ultimately, they are the ones whose systems, institutions, and citizens stand to benefit, or not.
Most of the time, these two clients want the same thing. But not always. When their priorities diverge — when what the funder wants differs from what government actually needs – a critical question emerges: who are we really serving? In our experience, this question is rarely asked explicitly. For smart TA to work, it needs to be.
#### The problem is structural, not intentional
This is not a story about bad intentions. The funders we work with are genuinely committed to supporting locally owned, government-led reform. But the way that TA is structured and commissioned can create incentives that unintentionally pull focus away from government — even when everyone involved wants the opposite.
A programme designed around fixed objectives or negotiated without deep understanding of what government actually needs, is already at a disadvantage. Deliverables that made sense at the appraisal or business case stage may look very different eighteen months later — when the context has shifted, a new minister is in place, or an unexpected window of opportunity opens. A TA team that feels bound to deliver what is in the contract, rather than responding to new needs or opportunities, risks closing that window rather than stepping through it.
The result is TA that is technically compliant but substantively disappointing. Reports and strategies are produced, workshops held, and boxes ticked. But little has changed. In our view, this is the opposite of smart TA — however well-intentioned the programme.
#### Centring government: what this means in practice
Smart TA, as we understand and practise it, starts from a different place. Our working definition — set out in full in our piece[ Built on site: How OPM delivers smart technical assistance](https://www.opml.co.uk/insights/built-site-how-opm-delivers-smart-technical-assistance) — describes smart TA as context-aware, politically informed, partnership-driven support that uses shared diagnosis and adaptive learning to build locally-owned, sustainable institutional capability. Every element of that definition points in the same direction: towards government, not towards the funder.
In practice, a focus on the TA recipient means several things. It means conducting diagnosis jointly with government counterparts rather than arriving with a ready-made theory of change. It means being politically literate enough to understand whose support a reform needs, and realistic enough to judge what pace of change is actually achievable. It means playing different roles at different moments — sometimes drafting the strategy directly, sometimes coaching government to do it themselves, sometimes acting as a broker between competing interests — and having the judgment to know which approach is needed when.
It also means being willing to advocate for the recipient when priorities are not aligned. This includes adapting terms of reference to more accurately reflect what government needs, or what is realistically possible as reform objectives. Where appropriate we make the case for pursuing reform opportunities that were not part of the original design. 
We use our position as an honest broker between donor and recipient — not as a passive implementer of whatever the contract specifies. The best funder relationships we have are the ones where that kind of honest brokerage is not just tolerated but actively welcomed.
#### What this has looked like on the ground
We found ourselves repeatedly in this position delivering the Pakistan Sub-National Governance (SNG) programme, where we provided embedded support to federal and provincial governments. At times we identified gaps between what the log frame specified and what government needed in reality — and where, working closely with FCDO, we were able to make the case for doing something different. Reform opportunities emerged — in areas such as subsidy policy and social protection reform — that were not in the original design. FCDO's willingness to pursue them with us, adapting the programme to what government’s real needs, is a large part of why those reforms moved at all.
The wheat subsidy reform in Punjab provides a concrete example. For years, a poorly targeted subsidy had created a fiscal drain and circular debt of around £2 billion. The political economy was deeply entrenched, with farmers, flour millers, banks, and parts of government all invested in maintaining the status quo. 
The turning point came during a Caretaker Government period when a political window briefly opened. We moved quickly – mapping liabilities, building an evidence base, coordinating action across finance, food, banking, and the private sector, and managing resistance from those who stood to lose. When the elected government returned, we worked to secure continuity and sustain political commitment through the transition. The result was the elimination of £2 billion in legacy debt, £750 million in savings, and fiscal space reallocated towards more targeted social protection. 
It is a story of working at the boundary between funder and TA recipient, and what becomes possible when that boundary is navigated with flexibility, trust, and a shared focus on what government actually needs. 
#### What good commissioning makes possible
None of this is an argument against accountability, rigour, or results frameworks. It is an argument for designing TA in a way that keeps government — and ultimately the citizens they serve — at the centre.
The best-commissioned TA programmes we have worked on share the following characteristics:
  * They are designed with genuine government input from the outset. 
  * They understand and incorporate local context.
  * They incorporate flexibility to adapt scope and pursue emerging opportunities.
  * They define success in terms of what changes on the ground, not what gets produced on paper.
  * They trust the TA provider to say, when necessary, "this is not what government or the TA recipient needs right now" — and are secure enough in the relationship to hear it.


That last point matters more than it might seem. A TA provider that only ever delivers what is asked of it is providing a service. There is nothing wrong with that. But it is not what reform requires — and it is not what we mean by smart TA.
Smart TA, done well, is a genuine three-way partnership: funder, consultant, and recipient, each playing their role. But it only works if everyone is clear from the outset about what the programme ultimately exists to do — and who it exists to serve. For us, that answer has always been TA recipient first.
