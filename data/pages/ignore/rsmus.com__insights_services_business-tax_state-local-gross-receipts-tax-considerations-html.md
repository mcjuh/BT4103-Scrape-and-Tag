# Gross receipts tax: 10 considerations for multistate businesses
### Key risks involving nexus, sourcing, classifications and compliance
September 02, 2026

Nexus, sourcing and classification rules often require separate analysis.

## Gross receipts tax compliance starts with understanding the risks
State and local gross receipts taxes can create significant exposure for multistate businesses because they generally apply to revenue rather than profit.
Although the applicable rates may appear modest, the combination of a broad tax base, limited deductions, multiple layers of taxation and jurisdiction-specific rules can produce a meaningful cost, including for businesses operating at low margins or at a loss. These taxes also extend beyond well-known state regimes to local business license taxes, industry-specific taxes and hybrid systems.
Gross receipts taxes often operate differently from income taxes and sales taxes, with their own nexus, sourcing and classification rules. Because responsibility may be split across tax, finance, legal and operations functions, obligations can be overlooked.
The following 10 considerations can help businesses identify potential exposures and strengthen compliance processes.
### **1. Recognize gross receipts taxes as a separate category of risk**
A gross receipts tax is generally imposed on revenue rather than net income. Deductions for the cost of goods sold, operating expenses and losses may be limited or unavailable. Consequently, even a low stated rate can produce a substantial liability when applied to a large receipts base, particularly for low-margin businesses.
Evaluating the effective tax burden, rather than relying on the stated rate, can give businesses a more accurate view of the cost by accounting for the size of the receipts base and the limited availability of deductions.
### **2. Understand where your liability may exist**
Broad-based state-level regimes exist in states like Delaware, Nevada, Ohio, Oregon, Tennessee, Washington and Texas. That list, however, should not define the boundaries of a review.
Local taxes, industry-specific taxes and taxes labeled as business license fees can also operate on gross receipts. Virginia’s business, professional and occupational license (BPOL), for example, illustrates how obligations can arise outside a conventional state return calendar.
Reviewing every state and locality where the business has employees, remote workers, property or other recurring contacts can help identify filing obligations before they become audit exposures or lead to unexpected assessments.
### **3. Account for hybrid and industry-specific regimes**
Not every receipts-based tax fits neatly within a traditional gross receipts tax label. For example, Hawaii’s general excise tax and New Mexico’s gross receipts tax may resemble sales taxes on an invoice, but the business remains the taxpayer and the liability mechanics differ from those of a conventional sales tax.
Industry-specific deductions, resale requirements, tax-on-tax treatment and customer exemptions can further affect the result.
Understanding who bears the legal liability for the tax can help businesses avoid underpayments, billing disputes and compliance positions that are difficult to defend on examination.
### **4. Assign clear ownership and escalation responsibilities**
Gross receipts tax obligations are frequently missed because no function clearly owns them. Some regimes are treated as indirect taxes, while others are managed with income or franchise taxes. Local notices may bypass both groups.
Clear ownership can reduce the risk that registrations, notices or filing obligations fall between departments and remain undiscovered until an audit or transaction due diligence review.
### **5. Analyze nexus separately for each tax and jurisdiction**
Gross receipts tax nexus standards are not uniform. Some jurisdictions apply economic thresholds based on in-state receipts, while others rely solely on physical presence.
The standards may also differ between a state tax and local taxes within that state. Employees, remote workers, property, equipment, representatives and agents may create physical presence even when the business has no traditional office.
A separate nexus analysis for each gross receipts tax regime can help businesses identify registration and filing obligations that may exist even when the same activities do not create an income tax or sales tax obligation.
### **6. Do not rely on Public Law 86-272**
Public Law 86-272 generally limits a state’s ability to impose a net income tax when specified conditions are satisfied. It does not generally protect a business from gross receipts taxes.
A company may therefore have a protected income tax position while remaining subject to a gross receipts tax on the same activity. 
Evaluating gross receipts tax registration, filing and accrual obligations separately can reveal exposure that is not apparent from the company’s state income tax filing position.
### **7. Classify each revenue stream under the applicable rules**
Many gross receipts tax regimes apply different definitions and rates to retailing, wholesaling, manufacturing, services and specialized activities. A classification based on a company’s general industry may not accurately describe each revenue stream.
Classifying revenue at the appropriate level can improve the accuracy of the tax calculation and help support the treatment applied to receipts if it is questioned.
### **8. Develop a defensible sourcing methodology**
Sourcing is often the most operationally demanding component of gross receipts tax compliance. Rules may differ for services, tangible goods, digital products, licenses and other receipts.
A benefit-received method may apply to one revenue stream or jurisdiction but not another. Using a single customer-address field or enterprise-wide sourcing rule can therefore create both underpayments and overpayments.
Sourcing may need to be treated differently across jurisdictions. A documented sourcing methodology can improve consistency, support audit defense and reduce exposure.
### **9. Evaluate pyramiding and intercompany charges**
Gross receipts taxes may apply on multiple transactions as value moves through a supply chain or between legal entities. Intercompany management fees, shared-service charges and similar payments may remain subject to a gross receipts tax even when eliminated in consolidated financial statements. 
Modeling the tax across the full operating structure can reveal costs associated with intercompany transactions and other internal flows that may not be apparent when reviewing only customer-facing revenue.
### **10. Maintain audit, diligence and change-management readiness**
Contracts, workpapers, internal memoranda, revenue mappings and documentation of nexus, sourcing, classifications, deductions and exemptions can help a business explain and defend its gross receipts tax positions during an examination. Documentation is particularly valuable for preferential rates and nontaxable positions that may receive greater scrutiny.
The same discipline supports mergers and acquisitions. Unresolved gross receipts tax liabilities may result in escrows, purchase price adjustments and post-closing remediation.
Prioritizing jurisdictions and revenue streams based on receipts, footprint and risk can focus resources on the areas with the greatest potential exposure. A recurring monitoring process can also help businesses identify when changes in their operations or applicable rules create new obligations.
## How tax advisors can support gross receipts tax compliance
Gross receipts taxes should not be treated as incidental license fees or simplified substitutes for income taxes. Their broad bases, limited deductions, local variations, and distinct sourcing and nexus rules can create material exposure even when rates appear low.
Businesses that have not recently evaluated their gross receipts tax footprint may benefit from a focused review of nexus, taxable receipts, classifications, sourcing methodologies and filing obligations. Advisors can help identify potential exposures, evaluate planning opportunities, support compliance and strengthen audit readiness as business operations evolve.
A structured approach built around ownership, jurisdictional analysis, reliable data, documented positions and early transaction planning can convert a frequently overlooked obligation into a manageable component of the state and local tax function.
