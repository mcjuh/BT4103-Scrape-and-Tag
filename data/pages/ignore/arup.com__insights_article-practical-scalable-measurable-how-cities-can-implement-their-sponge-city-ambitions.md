#  Practical, scalable, measurable: how cities can implement their sponge city ambitions 
Over the past decade, the concept of the ‘sponge city’ – environments designed to absorb, store and reuse water like a sponge – has been widely hailed as a workable approach to tackle flooding, water scarcity, pollution and urban heat. So why today do too many cities still struggle to design, implement and achieve the resilience that ‘sponginess’ promises?
Joe Shuttleworth
Associate, Digital Water

## What it takes to deliver sponge measures in real cities
When sponge city principles meet the complexity of real cities, the challenges quickly become apparent. Washington, D.C. provides a useful example. Faced with long-standing issues of combined sewer overflows and looming obligations under the Clean Water Act, DC Water embarked on the Clean Rivers Project, a multi-billion-dollar programme initially built around large underground tunnels designed to capture excess stormwater. As the project evolved, green infrastructure was introduced alongside these assets, allowing the utility to reduce the size of some tunnels and achieve both environmental and cost benefits.Yet implementation was far from straightforward. In a dense urban environment, decisions are shaped not only by engineering feasibility, but also by space, constructability and public acceptance. As Rabia Chaudhry, Director of Water Supply Resilience at DC Water, reflects: “while communities broadly supported the idea of green infrastructure, resistance often emerged when specific changes were proposed in individual streets or neighbourhoods. In practice, less visible and intrusive interventions such as permeable pavements proved easier to deliver than more transformative changes such as raingardens.”These experiences make clear that implementing sponge city measures is not simply a design challenge. It requires a deep working knowledge of the existing physical, institutional and social realities of cities.
## Why funding sponge cities remains a tough sell
Financing is a persistent barrier. While the benefits of sponge city measures are widely recognised, the costs are often borne by a few organisations, typically utilities or municipalities, making the business case harder to establish. This creates a fundamental misalignment: the value generated by sponge city measures is distributed across society, ecosystems and the economy, but the mechanisms to capture and reinvest that value are still underdeveloped.
The issue is not necessarily a lack of capital, but the absence of clear pathways to direct investment towards projects that deliver measurable outcomes and alignment with public, private, ecosystem, and community needs.”
Will Sarni 
Practice Lead for Water and Nature at Earth Finance 
To date, funding models for sponge city projects tend to have a specific character. A leading example is again in Washington D.C., where green infrastructure was delivered through the US’ first Environmental Impact Bond. Similar approaches are emerging elsewhere; in Mansfield in the UK, for example, digital analysis helped build a robust business case for investment, while also informing the design and delivery of nature-based interventions. However, even where alternative financing models exist, such as through green bonds or blended finance, they remain the exception rather than the norm.
From a corporate perspective, there is acknowledgement that water stewardship is a shared responsibility, with companies increasingly investing in contributing to building healthy watersheds. However, translating that interest into consistent, scalable funding for urban interventions remains a work in progress. As Will Sarni, Practice Lead for Water and Nature at Earth Finance, notes: “the issue is not necessarily a lack of capital, but the absence of clear pathways to direct investment towards projects that deliver measurable outcomes and alignment with public, private, ecosystem, and community needs.”
## How technology enables proactive responses to water risks
To unlock investment and support confident decision-making, cities need assurance that interventions will deliver real outcomes. This is where digital insights are starting to make a practical difference.
[ Tool  NatureInsight  ](https://www.arup.com/services/digital-solutions/natureinsight/)
Digital tools (including our NatureInsight) now allow cities to map land use at scale, identify precisely where interventions will have the greatest impact, and test different scenarios before a single asset is built. By combining spatial analysis, flood modelling and machine learning, authorities can prioritise interventions based on local conditions, constraints and available budgets. Cities move from relying on broad assumptions towards more targeted, evidence-based choices.
New flood modelling tools are also improving the ability to predict how urban environments and systems will deal with extreme weather events, incorporating surface flows alongside drainage infrastructure and groundwater interactions. This ability to test scenarios in advance shifts decision-making from reactive responses towards more proactive, risk-informed investment.
[ Project  Mansfield sustainable drainage systems  In Mansfield, UK we have assessed entire urban catchments digitally, enabling local planners to understand both impact and cost before implementation. Learn more.  ](https://www.arup.com/projects/mansfield-sustainable-drainage-systems/)
From a technology provider perspective, this reinforces the importance of integrating the widest range of data points and factors. As Alexis de Kerchove, Senior Director for Sustainability at Xylem, points out: “sponge cities cannot rely on isolated green interventions alone, for example distributed rain gardens, swales or rain butts. They depend on the coordinated operation of green, blue and grey infrastructure, supported by data and sensing systems that allow cities to optimise performance over time.”
Together, these capabilities help move sponge cities from planning intent into operational reality.
## Overcoming the people barrier
Despite these advances, many of the biggest barriers to implementation remain human. Fragmented governance, competing priorities and differing stakeholder perspectives can all slow progress.
Communication and language can be issues too. Terms like ‘sponge city’ while recognised within the industry, can feel abstract or unclear to non-specialists. For communities, what matters is how interventions affect their immediate environment: how streets look, what’s changing, how spaces are used and how disruption is managed.
This disconnection between system-level vision and local experience can lead to resistance, even where there is likely broad support for the underlying goals. As a result, engaging stakeholders effectively requires more than presenting technical solutions. It demands early, meaningful dialogue and a willingness to adapt proposals in response to local concerns.
Equally, there is a need to broaden the conversation. Too often, discussions about water resilience are confined to utilities and water professionals, while key stakeholders such as municipalities, planners, developers and the surrounding community are less engaged. Without bringing all these groups into the process, implementation risks remain.
## Renewed ambitions, wider action
Taken together, these challenges point to a shift in how future sponge city schemes need to be approached. The future lies in integrated systems that combine infrastructure, technology and governance, tailored to local conditions.
This means recognising that sponge cities shouldn’t been seen as an isolated solution, but a framework for decision‑making. Progress depends on aligning green, blue and grey infrastructure with digital tools that enable better planning and performance management, while creating the institutional and financial structures that support collaboration.
To expand sponge city initiatives at scale we clearly need to adopt a different kind of thinking and practice. One that treats water not as a standalone issue but approaches it as a system that cuts across infrastructure, environment and society. For cities looking to take the next step, discussing these challenges with Arup experts can help clarify priorities, explore practical pathways, and turn sponge ambition into action.
